From ancient times, in every part of the world, there has been an attraction towards gems. Gems, with their captivating colours and brilliant lustre, irresistibly charm the mind, and this gift of nature is found in almost every country of the world in one measure or another. Gems not only enhance the beauty of ornaments, but it is also believed that divine power resides in them; and for this reason too, the common people are drawn towards gems.

**[p. 10]**

There is not the least doubt that the use of gems in the cure of diseases has been in vogue from ancient times, and they do possess the power to cure disease. The number of gems is quite large, but in India jewellers have given special recognition to only eighty-four gems. Of these eighty-four, nine — ruby, pearl, coral, emerald, pushparaga (topaz), diamond, sapphire, gomed and vaiduryamani (cat's-eye) — are established in the category of the Navaratna (nine gems). Of these nine gems, five — ruby, pearl, diamond, sapphire and emerald — occupy a special place and are considered Maharatnas (great gems); the rest are only ratnas (gems). Those gems other than these nine are considered minor gems (uparatnas). But in beauty and value, many gems called uparatnas are not inferior to those counted among the nine gems. In hardness, durability, rarity, lustre, sparkle, transparency or translucency, those gems are in no way behind.

Below we give, according to traditional recognition, a list of the eighty-four gems together with a very brief introduction. Further on, we shall give a detailed account of these gems and of other gems in common use.

### Preliminary Information

The recognition of gems has no relation to the growth of civilisation. In other words, the prestige of gems is not a consequence of the growth of civilisation. Certain valuable gems, which today occupy a special place in our eyes, were equally honoured in ancient times.

Scholars admit that the Rigveda is the very first book of the world. Western scholars have not been able to discover any book more ancient than this to this day. In many mantras of the Rigveda the word "ratna" is used. In the very first mantra of this great book, Agni is called "ratna dhatamam" ("best bestower of riches"). (*Agnimile purohitam yajnasya devam rtvijam / Hotaram ratnadhatamam — Rigveda 1.1.1.*) In our ancient religious and historical books, which include the Ramayana, Mahabharata and others, there is mention of gems. In the pictures of our gods, goddesses and great men we see them adorned with precious necklaces of gems and ornaments. In the Agni Purana, Garuda Purana, Devi Bhagavata and other ancient works there is a detailed description of the names of the various great gems and gems such as diamond, ruby, sapphire, emerald, etc., their places of origin, special characteristics, merits and demerits, their testing, auspiciousness and inauspiciousness, and so on. In the *Brihat Samhita* composed by Acharya Varahamihira, a clear account of the merits and demerits of gems is given. In the ancient Ayurvedic works, which include *Madhava Prakasha*, *Rasa Ratna Samuchchaya*, and *Ayurveda Prakasha*, the methods of preparing the bhasmas (ashes) and pistis (powders) of various gems and their uses are described. The great Sanskrit poets, Kalidasa and others, used the beauty of gems in giving comparisons to their characters. In the works on polity such as the Artha-shastra of Kautilya and the Shukra-niti there is also a detailed discussion of gems. Many ancient manuscripts relating to gems are available even now.

**[p. 14]**

In the Bible too there is a discussion of various gems at many places. Yes, it is true that in ancient times many gems were known by other names: for instance, today's *lapis lazuli* (lajwart) was known in ancient times as "sapphire," and *peridot* was called "topaz." Soft gems like rock crystal and amethyst were greatly honoured, because they could be easily polished and made charming in various ways. *Trina-mani* (amber), *vidruma* (coral) and *mukta* (pearl) were considered valuable gems in ancient times, and many superstitions and legends were current about them.

As happens even today, from time to time the fashion for some gems rose and for others declined. From ancient times India has been a treasury of gems, but in western countries too there was no lack of admirers of gems. According to the book *Precious and Semi-Precious Stones* written by Michael Weinstein, the wealthy Romans were very fond of gems. At that time beautiful engraving on hard gems became popular; in this work the greatest skill belonged to the craftsmen of Egypt. Famous historical figures such as Alexander the Great, Mithridates, Julius Caesar, Augustus and others were collectors of gems. Some of the gems from their collections, it is said, still exist today.

Gem science has advanced greatly, and now so much knowledge of gems has been gained that in country after country many books have been written on the subject. Our effort in this book will be to give abundant information about the valuable and semi-precious gems that have so far come to light.

**[p. 15]**

The most important thing to know about gems is this: apart from a handful of gems, all are mineral substances and are obtained from the bowels of the earth. Hence they are products of an inorganic process and are made of one and the same kind of matter. All have a definite chemical composition which can be written in a clear formula. Only those stones or mineral substances can be counted among valuable gems which are extremely beautiful to look at, are rare, and have sparkle and durability in them; and for this reason only diamond, ruby, sapphire, emerald and select costly opals or milky stones are counted among the precious gems. Because of its charming lustre and attractive form, the pearl too comes into the category of precious gems, although it is an organic product, not a mineral substance.

Among the semi-precious stones come those mineral substances which do not get a place in the category of valuable gems. But remember that being semi-precious does not mean that those gems hold no importance, or that they do not occupy an honoured place in the gem world. In this category are many beautiful, attractive stones which are used in ornaments with great enthusiasm. Some of their names are: zircon, lal (spinel), topaz, harit-mani (peridot), moonstone, chrysoberyl, tourmaline, amethyst or violet stone, peridot and aquamarine. In these stones there is hardness, beauty, lustre and durability in sufficient measure. After cutting and polishing, some of them begin to rival the stones that come in the category of valuable gems. Especially, we have seen with our own eyes that peridot and aquamarine sometimes defeat emerald and sapphire with their lustre,

**[p. 16]**

and, to tell the truth, in the market they are usually sold under the name of valuable gems. Only an experienced and expert jeweller can recognise their real nature.

One more thing. In valuable and semi-precious gems, especially those we have discussed above, it does not seem entirely proper to distinguish them here and there. For example, diamond is a gem that comes in a valuable category, but it also happens that a low-grade diamond is sold for less than a beautiful, well-cut aquamarine or alexandrite. In this way there are also examples where an ordinary-grade emerald necklace was sold for a few pounds, while the value of a jade necklace made in China reached twenty-five thousand pounds.

Apart from valuable and semi-precious gems, there are two more categories of stones. One is the synthetic stone. These stones are not obtained from mines; they are made. In making them, the elements of real gems are so mixed that once prepared, their composition and form and colour are just like the real gem. In the second category come artificial stones, which in ordinary language are called "imitations." Synthetic stones are more durable than artificial ones. Artificial "gems," however much they may assume the same form and colour, are made of glass or plastic. In making opaque artificial "gems," mostly plastic has come to be used.

Apart from synthetic and artificial gems, one more category of gems is also made, in which two stones are joined to make one gem. This is called a doublet. This process is usually used to make a real gem of poor appearance full-sized and attractive. Its upper part is of the real gem, and the lower part of the same colour is fake. Usually the lower fake part is kept of a slightly deeper colour so that the colour of the real gem comes out richly. In the same way gems with three parts are also made. In the process of making them, the upper and lower parts are of the real gem, and in the middle a fake stone (glass, etc.) is inserted. By this process the colour of the gem improves on both sides.

**[p. 17]**

Emeralds, sapphires, rubies, opals and many other gems prepared in this way are usually seen in the jeweller's market.

Now science has advanced so far that representative synthetic stones of most valuable gems have begun to be made, but because of the high cost of production, synthetic stones of large size are not made. A French chemist named Moissan was the first person to succeed in making synthetic diamonds. He applied heat by means of an electric furnace to a mixture of iron and pure carbon. After keeping it at a temperature of four thousand degrees centigrade for some time, the mixture was thrown into cold water. Because of this sudden cooling, on the expanding outer surface all around the liquid substance an iron crust-like layer formed. The pressure that resulted was so great that some carbon that came out took the form of diamonds. But in this the process of separating the carbon and the iron was extremely difficult. Hence, although this experiment was considered very important from the scientific point of view, from the commercial point of view it proved of little benefit. The chemists named Hahn and Friedlander also prepared synthetic diamonds, but their effort too could not achieve much success from the commercial point of view. Synthetic diamonds have not yet been able to enter the jeweller's market.

There is production of synthetic stones of sapphire, topaz and ruby in sufficient quantity, and they are used in ornaments. These stones have one and the same chemical composition, alumina, and in their natural state come in the corundum class. The difference in their colours is due to the metallic oxides mixed in them. Full success in this matter was achieved in the year 1904. That year a French chemist, Verneuil, succeeded in preparing synthetic ruby, sapphire and topaz in the laboratory, and when the gems thus produced came into the jeweller's market, there was a fall in the value and sale of natural gems. Before this production of synthetic gems in the laboratory, this same chemist had succeeded in converting the powder of worthless natural rubies into the form of ruby.

**[p. 18]**

Although these synthetic stones have succeeded in making their place in the jeweller's market, an expert jeweller can recognise the difference between synthetic and natural stones. Hence even now these synthetic stones have not been able to get the prestige and honour that natural gems enjoy. In fact, the situation now is that because of the rarity of natural gems, their value has gone up even more than before.

After success was achieved in making synthetic stones imitating ruby, sapphire and topaz, synthetic stones imitating other gems also began to be made. Among these, the production of spinel and alexandrite is more important. They are used considerably in ornaments. But an expert jeweller can always easily recognise synthetic and natural stones with practice and the help of a microscope.

In this connection, the following facts are worth attention:

(a) In synthetic stones small air-bubbles are seen, which are usually perfectly round. If bubbles occur in natural stones, they are of irregular shape, and their form and colour is just like that of natural stones.

(b) In synthetic stones the fibres are formed in curvature. In natural stones the fibres are of various sizes and are scattered irregularly.

(c) If internal streaks exist, in natural stones they are straight. In synthetic stones they are usually curved.

(d) The internal optical effect that appears in ruby, sapphire and topaz, which is called "silk," never appears in synthetic stones.

(e) The colour of synthetic stones is usually not right. It is uniform, and there is a glass-like shine in it. In natural rubies,

**[p. 19]**

sapphires, etc., there is some difference in the colour of the various parts of the gem, and if streaks appear, they are parallel or irregular, never curved.

(f) If synthetic and natural gems are immersed in a solution of methylene iodide, the natural gem will sink and the synthetic will begin to float on the surface.

Most synthetic stones are prepared in Germany and France. In Italy, Switzerland and Russia too they are produced.

Those gems (in fact it would not be proper to call them gems) which are completely artificial are made of various kinds of glass, and on them there is a coating of a lead-silver alloy. In good quality artificial gems the quantity of lead oxide is quite high, which increases the shine of the glass; but this shine quickly fades away, whereas the sparkle of natural gems is quite lasting. In artificial gems, with the help of metallic oxides, various colours are filled; but these colours are not like the colours of natural gems.

In the year 1656, a man named Jacquin of the city of Paris made artificial pearls filled with wax. According to the process he introduced, inside round glass beads a silver-coloured paint was applied and then wax was filled in. But artificial pearls of this kind are no longer in vogue. Now solid artificial pearls have begun to be made. Japan has taken the foremost place in this field. There is not the least difficulty in recognising artificial and natural stones. These solid artificial pearls feel perfectly smooth to the touch, whereas in natural pearls there is not so much smoothness. If the point of some sharp thing is pressed against the surface of an artificial pearl, it will sink in a little. This will not happen on a natural pearl. If a hole is drilled in a pearl, whatever artificial substance is filled inside it becomes visible at the place of the hole. The real difficulty is in recognising natural and cultured pearls. We shall discuss this in the section relating to pearls.

### Origin

The origin of a gem is either from the bowels of the earth, where they slowly form, or they are obtained far away from their place of birth. The gems that come in the first category are parts of those rocks in which they take birth. Those gems found far from their place of birth are the ones which, separated from their parent rocks by weather, heat, cold and rain, are carried away with rivers, streams and other water sources and go far off. Rolling along and being rubbed in many ways, when these gem-stones are found in the beds of rivers, they become round or oval in shape. Their surface becomes smooth and their edges or roughness is worn away by continuous rubbing. In this way, in the gravel-beds of rivers, which are found in abundance in Sri Lanka, various kinds of gem-stones are found, and in separating them from other attached or joined substances there is not so much difficulty as there is in obtaining those gem-stones which

**[p. 21]**

form parts of hard rocks. Among these some gem-stones are such that sometimes they are not embedded in rocks, but are lodged in their cracks or holes. These stones too form a definite shape of their own and assume the form of crystals.

Now it is necessary to know what is the difference between a gem-stone and a crystal. A gem-stone is that which is joined to rocks, and when separated it has no definite shape. A crystal is that gem-stone which is a solid substance having a definite shape; all around its surface is smooth; its interior forms a definite framework. Its outward form is really the shape of its inner construction, and looking at this very fact we gain the knowledge of the difference between a natural gem-stone and a gem made of fake glass. Whereas a natural crystal grows larger by the continual joining of smaller crystal particles of the same shape, artificial glass is not prepared in this way. Crystals are divided into six methods or systems — the cubic system, the tetragonal system, the hexagonal group, the rhombic (orthorhombic) system, the monoclinic system, and the triclinic system.

It is an amusing natural coincidence that whatever gem-stones are obtained in the form of crystals, in whatever part of the world they may be found, there will be no difference in their shape. Emeralds will always be found of hexagonal shape, whether they are obtained in India or in Egypt and South America.

In this section we are not giving an account of the places of origin of the gems and gem-stones. That will be appropriate to give together with each particular kind of crystal. But one amusing thing is worth noting. It is this, that the gem-stones of some kinds are found together as well. For instance, ruby is usually found with spinel, and diamonds with tamra (a reddish stone). If a ruby is found, a search is also made for spinel; and where tamra is found, diamond is also looked for. This should be understood as a peculiarity of nature, because the chemical composition of these gem-stones is not the same.

**[p. 22]**

**Chemical composition of gem-stones.** If a chemical analysis or examination is made of all gem-stones, we shall find that the following elements play an important part in them: silicon, aluminium, iron, carbon, oxygen, hydrogen, copper, calcium, magnesium, sodium, zirconium, beryllium and phosphorus.

Except diamond, which is absolutely pure carbon, other gem-stones are a mixture of two or more of the above elements. Take, for example, quartz. It is an oxide of silicon, and in it oxygen and silicon are mixed in a definite proportion. Here it is also necessary to state that the colour of any gem-stone has no connection with its chemical composition. The cause of the colour is the influence of some organic or inorganic substance in that gem-stone.

**Hardness and cleavage.** If the gems used in ornaments etc. were not hard, there would be no durability in them. Hardness means the capacity of the gem not to break or crack. By scratching one gem against another, their mutual ratio of hardness can be estimated. The gem which makes a scratch on another gem will be considered harder than it. The order of hardness of various gem-stones in comparison with one another, estimated according to Mohs' scale, is given in the table below. How much hardness there is in various gem-stones, we shall state in the context of each gem:

1. Talc or soapstone; 2. Gypsum or chalk; 3. Calcite; 4. Fluor-spar; 5. Apatite; 6. Feldspar; 7. Quartz (in which the gem-stones of the rock-crystal family are included); 8. Topaz;

**[p. 23]**

9. Corundum-family gem-stones such as ruby, sapphire etc.; 10. Diamond.

Whichever gem-stone's hardness is to be found out, it should be tested by scratching it with some mineral of the Mohs' scale. If the mineral being tested scratches that mineral of the above scale, it will be harder than it. But it should be clearly understood that the quantities of this scale-system have no significance. In fact, the difference between the hardness of diamond and ruby is greater than that between ruby and talc. Remember that sand will have no effect on quartz or gem-stones harder than it, but on glass a scratch will easily be made with it. The hardness of ordinary glass is about 5.5. Similarly, the hardness of gypsum is so low that a scratch can be made on it even with a fingernail. One more thing needs to be known. It is this, that the hardness of gem-stones from two different mines of the same family of one mine is more or less. This is especially seen in diamond, and it has also been observed that in different parts of one and the same gem-stone the hardness is more or less. The reason for this is the internal development of their construction.

**Cleavage** means the capacity of a gem-stone to break or to be cut with special ease in a particular part or portion, as a result of which its separated surface remains smooth. Great importance is attached to cleavage in gem-stones. Without it their cutting etc. cannot be done with ease and cleanliness. In diamond, especially, cleavage is found very good despite its hardness.

**Specific gravity or density.** Specific gravity means the weight of a substance in comparison with pure water at a temperature of four degrees centigrade. A solid substance is compared with one cubic centimetre of water, whose weight is one gram. By this ratio, one cubic centimetre of carnelian would weigh 2.46 grams. Hence there is variation in their specific gravity, which we shall explain in the context of every gem-stone.

**[p. 24]**

There are several ways to find out the specific gravity of gem-stones. A simple method for small stones is to immerse them in a liquid of known specific gravity. If the stone begins to float on the surface, its specific gravity will be less than that of the liquid. If it remains sunk, its specific gravity will be greater than that of the liquid.

The liquids usually used in this connection are bromoform (specific gravity 2.9), methylene iodide (specific gravity 3.32), Klein's solution (specific gravity 3.28), Sonstadt's solution (specific gravity 3.18), and Retgers' solution (specific gravity 4.6). Of these liquids, the use of the first and second is very easy, and mostly these two are used.

But be careful. Some gem-stones such as opal and turquoise are porous. Their examination should not be done in any liquid other than water.

**Electrical power in gem-stones.** There are some gem-stones in which electrical power is produced on being heated. Among these the main ones are topaz, quartz, diamond and tourmaline. In amber too, on rubbing, negative electrical power arises.

**The effect of heat on gem-stones.** Some gem-stones such as zircon and topaz are heated with care to bring out their colour. But if too much heat reaches them, most gem-stones are spoilt. Opal cracks and its lustre is lost. The colour of turquoise also runs off. In tourmaline electrical power arises. Amethyst becomes yellow, and many quartz-family gem-stones turn brown or yellow.

**[p. 25]**

Some grey stones take on the appearance of carnelian. But zircon becomes white and its brilliance increases. At very high temperature diamond is converted into the form of black carbon. Some topazes lose their colour, some become pinkish-red. Ruby and some other stones are those which lose their colour from excessive heat, but on cooling regain their original colour. Pearls become brownish and break, and amber begins to burn, giving out the smell of camphor. It is believed that if the colour of a gem-stone is due to some organic substance mixed in it, heat destroys its colour. If the colour in a gem-stone is due to some inorganic substance, then on getting cool it usually regains its original colour.

**Optical properties of gem-stones.** These properties are of great importance, because by means of them the special testing of various stones is done. In this connection scientific knowledge of light is extremely necessary, but without going into detail we shall confine ourselves to our subject. Since the structure of gem-stones is crystalline, light has a great effect on them, and therefore their optical properties are of very great importance.

Many people hold the notion that examining a gem-stone by its colour is sufficient, but the strange and true fact is that the more experience one has in this connection, the more wrong this test turns out. In white light there are seven colours in different proportions — red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo and violet. These seven colours become clearly visible separately when the image of white colour is thrown through a prism. When white light comes into contact with any gem-stone, some light is absorbed in its surface and some passes through the stone. On this very action the true colour of the gem-stone is based. Of these seven colours, some the gem-stone absorbs well, and for some the process of absorption is limited. Some minerals can absorb only some particular colours, hence in passing through them there arises an inequality among the colours. It also happens that in one and the same stone

**[p. 26]**

there is variation in the process of colour absorption. This is the reason that some stones are such that if they are seen from different edges or angles, different kinds of colours appear in them. This is found especially in tourmaline. This property depends upon the atomic structure of the gem-stone.

When a ray of light falls on a medium such as a gem-stone, it becomes refracted, that is, it bends from its normal path (existing in a straight line), and this bent ray makes something of an angle with the normal path, which remains constant and the same for every stone of one kind. It is by the measurement of this angle of refraction that the stone is identified. According to scientific opinion, this is the most certain method of identifying the genuineness of a stone. From the measurement of this process of refraction the refractive index of each gem-stone is determined. For this kind of measurement an instrument is available which is called a refractometer.

Here it is worth noting that many gem-stones have more than one refractive index. The gem-stones of the cubic group and the amorphous gem-stones, which include opal and garnet, have only one refractive index. In transparent gem-stones the optical ray is refracted in more than one direction, therefore they have more than one refractive index. These refractive indices can be easily determined with a refractometer.

We have stated above that from some gem-stones, such as tourmaline, more than one colour appears when seen from different angles. This property is called dichroism.

For testing this property an instrument is also available, which is called a dichroscope. There can be no dichroism in the cubic-group stones or artificial stones. From this single property alone the difference between garnet and tourmaline can be recognised. Similarly, for coloured stones, a list of dichroic stones and their refractive indices is given:

The breaking up of white light by a gem-stone and, by that process, the creation of a spectrum is called dispersion, and by this capacity its brilliance is determined. This brilliance appears with great clarity in diamond, sphine (sphalerite?) and zircon after cutting.

**[p. 28]**

On some gem-stones a charming miracle of colours is seen, and on some colours rest as if sprinkled with lustre and sparks of stars. The reason for this is their peculiar internal construction, which obstructs the spread of light. We shall give a detailed account of this at suitable places.

**(a) Gem-stones and Röntgen rays (X-rays).** Röntgen rays are those special electric rays which are used to see the interior of a solid substance. They are also called "X-rays." By these rays also great help is obtained in recognising genuine and fake gem-stones. When these rays fall on diamond, amber and jade, they appear perfectly transparent. By this method, diamond is easily recognised from other white gem-stones or glass. In the same way, ruby and sapphire too can be clearly distinguished by the use of these rays from other gem-stones of their colour. When these rays are thrown on opal and chrysoberyl, they appear less transparent. The stones of the quartz family, feldspar and topaz appear semi-transparent on the falling of these rays. Turquoise, tourmaline, peridot and apatite appear almost opaque. Almandine garnet, beryl, zircon and all kinds of glass appear completely opaque on the falling of these rays.

A German scientist named Walter first began the process of testing gem-stones by Röntgen rays.

The object of our writing on this subject in some detail is that gem-stones should not be identified only by their colour and lustre (which can prove to be a deceiving method), but it is necessary to recognise them also by their other properties. For examining unpolished and uncut stones, it is necessary to see their crystalline form, hardness, specific gravity and colour. While examining cut gem-stones,

**[p. 29]**

it is proper to check their refractive index, specific gravity, dichroism, hardness, colour, etc. The meaning of what we say, in brief, is that in trying to reach any conclusion by looking at only a couple of properties of gem-stones, there will always remain the possibility of being deceived.

**(b) Cutting, colouring and weighing of gems.**

The glittering, charming gems that we see set in ornaments are quite different from their original form. Such an attractive form is obtained only after their beautiful cutting and polishing. Their natural shape is irregular and shapeless. Sometimes they are obtained in the form of pebbles and sometimes in the form of pieces of broken crystals or stones. Time and weather make their shape and outward form strange, and the beauty and inner lustre of their polish is seen only when their cutting and polishing has been done. This work requires great skill. Those minerals which have become extremely defective are not converted into the form of gems. In cutting, full attention is paid to the hardness and cleavage of the gem-stone, and with the development of experience and proficiency in this direction, the cutting of gem-stones has begun to be done in various shapes and proportions.

Although now the cutting of gem-stones in India has begun to be quite good, the most beautiful and clean cutting is done in Holland, Belgium, Germany and France. The best cutting of jade is considered to be that of China. Antwerp and Amsterdam are the chief centres of diamond-cutting. In India, in Jaipur and Bombay too diamonds are cut. For the cutting of other valuable and semi-precious gem-stones, there are the places named Idar and Oberstein in Germany. There, these stones are also coloured and synthetic stones are also prepared. In London too cutting work is done, but this work is confined to gem-stones of the best quality. In South Africa this work was started, but no special progress could be made in it. Yes, in India there is daily progress in this direction, and it is hoped that

**[p. 30]**

within a few days our country will obtain a special place in this field.

In the big cutting establishments nowadays, electrical power is used for the work of cutting, but the work of fineness and great cleanliness is done only with hand-operated machines or tools. First, pieces are made of gem-stones with the vertical force-driven (power) wheels, then they are rubbed with wheels running with horizontal force. For polishing diamonds these wheels are made of soft iron. Different kinds of polishing and smoothing materials are used, and a hard grade of diamond, which is called bort, is used for rubbing and polishing the harder stones. In coloured stones the facets (angles) are not quite as perfect as in diamond, because in the cutting of coloured stones the chief object is to bring out their real colour in the best possible way, and at the same time to maintain the suitable weight of the gem-stone.

The cutting of gem-stones is done in several ways. Here we give a brief account of the principal cuts:

(1) **Cabochon cut.** Before the prevalence and development of the art of cutting gems, natural gem-stones, which are called "rough," were usually rounded and made by grinding. In most gems a hole was made and they were strung in the form of a rosary and used. By the cabochon cut, or the convex cabochon cut, the lentil-shaped cut, the high cut, the simple or plain, and the hollow or concave-convex are made. The outer outline of a cabochon-cut gem can be circular, elliptical or oval. In the double cabochon cut both the upper and lower surfaces of the gem are convex. If the curvature of both is alike, it is called the "lentil-shaped cut." In the high cabochon shape, on cutting the gem-stone, its upper part becomes very high. In the plain cut the lower surface is flat. In the hollow cut, the lower surface is made hollow and therefore becomes concave; in it the upper part remains convex.

**[p. 31]**

Opal, moonstone and cat's-eye are usually seen in cabochon cut. Garnet gem-stones are cut in hollow cut; from that their colour brightens. Turquoise and tourmaline too are usually seen in this kind of cut.

(2) **Brilliant cut.** The brilliant cut is so much in vogue for diamond that this gem itself has come to be called "brilliant." This cut is also called "the complete cut." This cut has also been considered suitable for artificial gems. In it there are altogether 58 facets. 33 facets are towards the crown (face) and 25 towards the pavilion (point). A definite proportion is necessary among these facets. The angle between the crown-side facets and the girdle should be 40 to 7 degrees [sic — 40° to 41°]. Similarly the angle between the pavilion-side facets and the girdle should be 40 degrees. In a well-made gem, the height of the crown should be one-third of the total height, and the width of the girdle (table) should not be more than four-fifths of the total width of the gem.

(3) **Table cut.** In this cut, the natural octahedron's upper and lower points of the gem-stone (especially diamond) are made smooth and polished. The upper facet is called the "table," and the lower facet is much wider than the culet.

(4) **Rose cut.** This method of cutting is used only for very small diamonds. The shape is a plain cabochon, but on the curved surface there are facets. Mostly there are 24 facets. This cut is suitable for those small gems which are set around some large gem.

(5) **Step cut.** This cut is usually done for emerald and topaz. The upper and lower facets are horizontal and parallel. There is no fixed rule as to what proportion they should be in.

(6) **Mixed cut.** In this method of cutting the upper part of the gem is of the brilliant cut and the lower part of another cut. Amethyst is mostly cut according to this method.

**[p. 32]**

Besides the cuts given above, many other shapes are also found in gems. Among these the main ones are: baguette, pointed triangles, epaulette, half-moon, cushion stones, kite, tablet, navette, pentagon, square, trapezium and triangle.

(7) **Net or cushion cut.** Gems of the emerald or tourmaline type are made in net cut or cushion cut. A stone of this kind is cut somewhat flat, and in it one or more rows of facets are set parallel to the girdle. Similar to this is the table cut, in which the table is very large and it meets the girdle at bevelled points. Sometimes the cut of the upper part of the gem differs from the cut of its back part.

(8) **Making gems.** In ancient times the process of cleaning gems and making beautiful gems was not as widespread as it is today. The ancient process was this: the powder of a stone harder than the stone from which the gem was being prepared was made and mixed with a base to prepare a lap. On that lap the gem was ground and prepared. Hence regular triangles etc. were not made.

Although diamond is the hardest, most durable and most valuable of all gems, yet in that age, for want of means, there was no process of making its good facets. Hence it could not get precedence; rather pearl and coral alone were considered pre-eminent.

(9) **Modern process.** The material which comes to the market after coming out of the mine is called rough. The rough should first be soaked in lemon and soda and cleaned, and if any foreign substance is stuck to it, it should be separated. Soaking new rough for two or three hours is sufficient. If the rough has been lying old, it will have to be soaked for twelve to twenty-four hours. Then it should be washed with water, cleaned and dried; after that, sesamum (til) oil should be applied to it, by which smoothness comes into it. From applying the oil, knowledge is gained of the condition inside the piece. Then throwing it on a table, it should be sorted according to the colour and water (quality), and by the proportion of light and dark colour and water, it should be divided into different lots. Of these lots,

**[p. 33]**

seeing piece by piece, it will be appropriate also to decide which pieces should be made into what. After that, the flaws etc. of the pieces should be seen and removed. For separating the flaws, in former times a diamond-point cutting tool was used, but nowadays, on account of greater facility, the cutting is done with a laser [OCR: "plas"]. It should also be kept in mind that the laser should be applied slowly only on the flaws or weak parts. Applying the laser with force risks the rough developing cracks and being spoiled.

In former times, cutting was done by tying a wire on a bow-shaped frame and using corundum powder, but nowadays cutting is done by a wheel. That cutting is considered beautiful in which no edge is made by the chisel, and it should also be kept in mind that while cutting, the chisel should be so used that its grain (direction) comes out properly in making the facet. The paste used in cutting is prepared by mixing dead horn, corundum and Multani clay (fuller's earth).

In former times, whatever rough came in front, its material was made just so. Today this matter is worth attention: that gems should be made according to the demand of the market, and in weight too as far as possible no great decrease should come.

After the above process the pieces will be ready for making facets, and can be given to the craftsman to make.

The lap on which the craftsman grinds them is made of corundum and leather. Nowadays foreign powder is also put in the lap. Although from this the grinding is done quickly, yet the elasticity does not remain the same. Stones of the soft kind are easily ground on a corundum lap, but for grinding hard stones like ruby, sapphire, etc., the lap is made very sharp by mixing blue [stone] powder in it, by which the rough of the hard stone is also easily and quickly ground and gems of good facet are made. By this method, in making rough, the elasticity and shine remain proper, and the loss of weight is also less.

**[p. 34]**

(10) The method of making diamond gems is different from other gems. In the complete cut (full cut), which is most in vogue for the gems of diamond and fake stones, there are only 58 facets. 33 facets are towards the crown, 25 facets towards the pavilion. A definite proportion is necessary among these facets. The angle between the crown-side facets and the girdle should be 40 to 41 degrees. Similarly the angle between the pavilion-side facets and the girdle should be 40 degrees. In a well-made gem, the height of the crown should be one-third of the total height, and the width of the girdle (table) should not be more than four-ninths [sic] of the total width of the gem.

After making the facets, it is also necessary to set them (sadhai). Setting means to see by what kind of arrangement the lustre will come in the gem. While setting a round or oval gem, first the "havela" (Persian) is set. That havela is divided into eight parts, which is called "adhtha." After this the facets are applied. These are twenty-four, and after this the base is polished. Afterwards polishing is done on the facets. Polishing is done with a fine iron needle. The needle is a kind of stone; it is burnt in fire and a needle is made of it. On the pavilion side, from the girdle to the point, steps are laid according to the proportion of its thickness. At least three steps each are necessary. In brilliant cut, steps are not laid. On the point, a flower-like arrangement is made. In the remaining parts then small notches are applied. Between the eighth and the table, a "umarel" (a fine line) is necessary. This umarel is made by applying a fine line of polish between the girdle and the notch on the base side. By this the beauty of the gem increases. Between the table and the eighth, notches are not applied as in the girdle; in it only notches are applied. On the pavilion side only long notches are applied; small notches as in the girdle are not applied.

In the pan and marquise cut, on the pavilion side a fine arrangement is made, but on the point, in place of the fine arrangement on the girdle side, the gem becomes beautiful and shining by applying two long notches. If on the point

**[p. 35]**

two small dots are applied, the shape of the pan will be spoiled. Therefore two small dots should not be applied.

The method of cutting old gems to make new ones is quite different from rough. First, two pieces should be made through the hole of the gem. Care must be taken that the gem does not become without direction (berukha) from the cutting. While cutting, it is also necessary to note whether the direction (grain) of the cutting comes or not. When the direction comes, there is ease in making the gem. While cutting the gem, care should also be taken that the part of lustre (the clear part) should not go away in the cutting. As far as possible, the coloured part should be saved from cutting. If the coloured part is cut, the colour will be less.

After the facets are made, polishing is done. On soft stones like emerald, polishing is done on a lap of tin or brass, and on hard stones like ruby, on a lap of copper. While polishing, the needle-paste is applied on the lap. The gems of soft stone are polished on a wooden piece (akta). This akta is made of the wood of ar, oak and crook. Before polishing, it is necessary to moisten only the gems of soft stone and the stones.

The drilling of gems is done by putting fine diamond dust on an iron wire.

After this, every material — gems, stones, tavra, etc. — after soaking in lemon juice mixed with soda for at least 12 hours, is taken out. Afterwards it is washed. While washing, remember that hot material should not be washed with cold water, because from cold water there is the possibility of the cracks breaking or new cracks appearing in the material. Hence it should be washed with hot water. After that, the material should be properly dried and given a coating (joban). The coating of emerald is prepared from veruja, jangal, Rummi mastagi and turmeric. The coating of ruby is prepared from ratanjot. Nowadays in place of ratanjot, the coating is prepared with red colour. By this coating the material is prepared well once, but soon begins to spoil. There are different processes of khara munavat etc.

**[p. 36]**

In principle, the process of making different stones is separate, but these processes that have been told are generally useful in the manufacture of all gems. Only in diamond is this speciality that it is ground only with diamond and is polished on a steel lap.

Nowadays tavra are made in patsan, on this the polish comes out well. It is a developed form of the earlier process.

**Filling colour in gems artificially.** The process of filling colour in gem-stones artificially is used to make their original form more attractive. With the increase in their beauty, their value also increases. This process is not new. According to the writings of Pliny, in ancient times jewellers were fully conversant with this process. Many of the methods adopted by them are no longer in vogue, and others have been improved.

In all porous gem-stones colour is filled with great success. But that colour is not lasting. In this process sufficient time is taken and great care is required, so that the gems are not spoilt. In it the gems are heated, and it is very important to take care that the temperature does not exceed the suitable limit. If the work of colouring is done in some mixed liquid chemical, it is necessary to take care that the gem-stone does not remain in the liquid for more than the suitable time. For this process the duration of time is separate for different stones.

Among cheap gem-stones, chalcedony, which is a semi-precious stone of the quartz family, does not come in the market without artificial colouring. In it a shining blue colour is filled, and those in which red colour is filled are sold in the market under the name of "roseleine." Both colours soon fade, and spots begin to appear here and there on the stone. Their colouring is done after cutting. In the agate-family gem-stones, the colour comes up by giving heat alone. Carnelians of shining red colour get such a coloured brightness by artificial heat. The natural colour of the stones of this family produced in India itself is good; there is no need of colouring them. Before giving heat, the solution of iron vitriol is made to reach inside the red stones. Green and

**[p. 37]**

blue-coloured agates are not so beautiful in their natural state. In them the beautiful blue colour is obtained by first putting them in a solution of ferric oxide and then of potassium ferrocyanide. In them the attractive green colour is obtained by putting them in a solution of nickel salt or chromic acid and giving heat. In them the yellow colour is obtained by giving gentle warmth in hydrochloric acid for about fifteen days.

It also happens that some agate stones have non-porous parts. In these parts artificial colour cannot penetrate. The result of this is that in such stones some white parts remain along with the colour. The colours applied on agate stones are mostly lasting, and for this reason these stones become extremely attractive after colouring in comparison with their natural state.

In the same way, in onyx the white and black streaks shine out by heat and chemical effect. The gem-stone named jasper is usually coloured blue, and afterwards it is sold in the market under the name of Swiss lapis or German lapis. There are various processes of bringing good blue colour in it; but none can give a lasting colour, nor can that colour rival the real lapis lazuli. Turquoise is also given artificial colour, but it is not lasting. When turquoises of good colour in their natural state lose their colour, then artificially coloured stones can keep their colour lasting in no way.

Because of the demand for old-style amber, newly obtained gem-stones are coloured. Good cut pieces are immersed in colour mixed with oil, and a little heat is given to them. Then the pieces are dried and cleaned. Because amber is extremely brittle, in this process many pieces get spoilt or their weight decreases. Their colours become attractive, but are not fully lasting. It is heard that by a new process success has been achieved in making the artificial colour given to amber lasting.

**[p. 38]**

In zircon, colour is less filled; their colour is more often removed. On heating they become colourless and their lustre increases. In fact, coloured zircons of this kind are usually sold in the form of diamond. But if attention is paid to its less hardness and greater specific gravity, there is no difficulty in knowing the difference between diamond and zircon.

Gem-stones of crystalline form, and topaz and other gem-stones, are improved by artificial process. We shall discuss this at the suitable place. But we can say this much, that most artificial improvements are temporary, and if such stones are examined with care and attention, the effect of the artificial process becomes clear. Here it is also necessary to state that many uncoloured gem-stones such as blue zircon, yellow topaz, pink quartz and amethyst lose their natural colour by exposure to strong sunlight.

**Weighing.** Nowadays the international trade of gems is done by the carat system, which is as follows:

100 cents = 1 carat; 58 carats = 1 bhari (tola); 5000 carats = 1 kilogram; 5 carats = 1 gram; 200 milligrams = 1 carat.

Now, although the carat system has come into vogue in Indian markets, still the name "ratti" has not been forgotten by either the customer or the shopkeeper.

In India the weights of gems at different places are as follows:

**Bombay:** quarter-ana-bhar = 1/64th of a ratti; half-ana-bhar = 1/32nd of a ratti; one-ana-bhar = 1 ratti; 24 ratti = 1 rtok; 2 ratti = 1 tola [sic]. In South India mostly the Bombay weight is in vogue.

**[p. 39]**

**Rajasthan:** 20 viswa = 1 ratti; 24 ratti = 1 tak; 85 ratti = 1 bhari (tola).

**West Bengal:** 20 viswa = 1 ratti; 24 ratti = 1 rtok; 84 ratti = 1 bhari (tola).

In Uttar Pradesh, Assam and Bihar mostly the Calcutta weight is in vogue.

**Inter-provincial difference of weights:** Bombay 200 ratti = 1 carat; Jaipur 100 ratti = 1/2 carat; Calcutta 100 ratti = 1/2 carat [sic].

**Various names.** Sanskrit: manikya, padmaraga, lohita, shonaratna, raviratna, shonotpala, kuruvinda, saugandhika, vasuretna. Hindi: chunni, rungal. English: ruby. Urdu-Persian: yaqut.

**Relationship of the nine gems to the nine planets.** As we have said before, according to Indian belief the nine gems are: (1) manikya (ruby), (2) moti (pearl), (3) munga (coral), (4) panna (emerald), (5) pukhraj (topaz), (6) heera (diamond), (7) neelam (sapphire), (8) gomed, and (9) vaidurya-mani (cat's-eye); the rest are minor gems.

The relation of these nine gems is respectively considered to be with (1) the Sun, (2) the Moon, (3) Mars, (4) Mercury, (5) Jupiter, (6) Venus, (7) Saturn, (8) Rahu and (9) Ketu. It is written in the *Jataka Parijata* that the gem of the Sun is ruby, of the Moon a clean pearl, of Mars coral, of Mercury garutmatka (emerald), of Jupiter pushparaga (topaz), of Venus diamond, of Saturn pure blue sapphire, of Rahu gomed and of Ketu vaidurya (cat's-eye).

**Ancient beliefs about their origin.** In connection with the origin of gems, many ancient beliefs and myths related to them are well known.

**[p. 41]**

According to the *Agni Purana*, for the slaying of the mighty Vritrasura, Lord Vishnu advised Indra to make a weapon from the bones of the great ascetic Dadhichi. On the prayers of the gods, the sage Dadhichi gave his body to the gods, and Indra, the king of gods, made the thunderbolt (vajra) from his bones and slew Vritra with it. While the weapon was being made, the bone-fragments that fell on the earth became the mines of diamond. In fact this legend only tells that there is nothing harder in the world than diamond.

**The story of King Bali.** According to another mythological story, Maharshi Parashara told in answer to the question of King Ambarisha that for slaying the demon-king Bali, Lord Vishnu assumed the Vamana (dwarf) avatar and crushed his pride. On that occasion, by the touch of the Lord's feet, Bali's whole body became full of gems. Then Indra, the king of gods, struck it with the thunderbolt. Thus, the gem-studded fragments of Bali scattered by breaking were taken by Lord Shiva on his trident, and making them the basis of the lordship of the nine planets and the twelve signs, he threw them down on the earth. From these fragments thrown on the earth, the mines of various gems were formed in the womb of the earth. It is said that from the various parts of King Bali's body alone the origin of the 84 kinds of gems, stones and jewels took place. As from blood arose ruby, from flesh (mansa) pearl [sic], from bile emerald, from flesh topaz, and so on.

**The story of Bala-asura.** In the Garuda Purana a similar story is told of Bala-asura. He defeated Indra in battle. Though he was a demon, he was a great giver and firm in his word. Indra took advantage of this virtue of his and deceived him. Disguised as a Brahmin, he prayed to him and asked that for the gods' sacrifice he would offer his body as a sacrifice (bali). When the body of Bala-asura, become an animal, was offered in fire, wherever his various limbs fell on the earth, there the mines of various gems were formed.

**The story of the churning of the ocean.** This too is described in the Puranas. According to it, when the gods and demons churned the ocean, fourteen gem-substances came out — among them the Kaustubha gem was one gem. Then there was a conflict between gods and demons over the nectar that came out. In the snatching and grabbing, wherever the drops of nectar fell, there, mixed with the earth's soil, various kinds of "gems" were formed.

**[p. 42]**

Acharya Varahamihira, keeping these stories in mind in the Brihat Samhita, has told as it were that some hold that gems originated from the body of the demon "Bala," some believe gems are formed from Dadhichi's bones, and the belief of some is that all gems were formed in the womb of the earth by the nature of the soil itself. He does not express any opinion of his own on this.

**Today's scientific age.** But today, the scientific man who travels to the moon and builds his home in space cannot accept these stories as they are. Today scientists have come to know in which mines what kinds of gems are found, and what their physical and chemical structure is. Therefore we have to believe that these stories and beliefs are narrated only to imprint the special qualities of gems on the reader's mind. These stories are narrated to show that in the diamond formed from Dadhichi's bones there is hardness, in the ruby produced from the body's blood there is the speciality of blood-like red colour, and so on.

Ruby and sapphire are gems of the corundum group. Chemically they are aluminium oxide. The granular gem-stone of corundum is called "emery." Their hardness is 9, specific gravity 4.03, refractive index 1.76–1.77, double refraction 0.008. Dichroism is strong.

Both ruby and sapphire are valuable and popular gems. Both are counted among the great gems (maharatnas). Ruby is of a beautiful red colour, sapphire of an attractive blue colour, and emery of black colour. The red sparkle of ruby and the splendid blue brilliance of sapphire are not to be found in any other gem. It is a miracle of nature that one and the same substance has colours so different. The crystals of ruby are mostly obtained in six-sided prisms and of sapphire in twelve-sided prisms. Both come under the hexagonal system. All gems are transparent or translucent and

**[p. 43]**

because of their hardness and being obtained in attractive and various colours, they occupy a special place in ornamental use. In this group the yellow stones that are obtained are called "yellow topaz." Those which are colourless are white topaz. The green gem-stones obtained are called "green topaz" or "Oriental emerald." The violet gem-stones become Oriental amethyst. Rose-coloured gem-stones are also obtained in the corundum group. The difference in colours is due to the metallic oxides contained in them. Star ruby and star sapphire are also obtained sometimes, and when they are cut in cabochon cut, a six-rayed star-like radiance appears at their summit. Sometimes different colours are also seen in one and the same gem-stone. In almost all gem-stones there is surely some defect or other.

The most valuable colour of ruby is that which is like pigeon's blood. Large and flawless gem-stones are rare, and if some are found, their value is very high. Most stones are defective, and in them sometimes defects like "silk" appear. It has been experienced that these silk-like defects occur in ruby, especially those obtained in Siam (Thailand), due to fine particles of "titanic iron." In dark-coloured gem-stones the quantity of iron oxide is more, and this is considered to be the cause of the red colour present in ruby.

A pigeon's-blood-red ruby, which is considered first grade, if of weight above two carats is considered rare and becomes extremely valuable. The real fact is that rubies of large size and greater weight are rarely seen. For rarity alone ruby has become more valuable than diamond. Not only now — even up to the sixteenth century, the then-famous jeweller Tavernier, who used to roam country and foreign countries in search of gems, remained unsuccessful in the search of a large-sized ruby.

Star ruby is among the extremely rare gems, and large-sized rubies are likewise rare. The obtaining of a ruby of ten carats' weight is

**[p. 44]**

considered almost impossible, and if by chance or good fortune a ruby of this weight of the best quality is obtained, it is of greater value than a diamond of the same weight (which is considered the most expensive gem). In 1919 a ruby of 42 carats was obtained which, in the form of rough, was sold for twenty-two thousand pounds. A cut ruby of seven and a half carats was sold in 1935 for ten thousand pounds.

In all the coloured stones of this group there is strong dichroism or doubleness, and it is necessary to pay attention to it in the cutting of the gem. Although these gem-stones are extremely hard, yet on the plane of cleavage the crystals break with great ease. One more thing: the hardness of all stones of this group is not the same. Rubies are somewhat less hard than sapphires. The specific gravity of all is almost the same, and its double refraction can be seen with the eyes themselves. Yes, the brilliance of colour is not great. From this single property alone, colourless corundum can be distinguished from diamond.

When ultraviolet rays are thrown on red or ruby, a very miraculous effect of colours is seen. The gem-stones or cut gems of ruby appear as if they are burning in brilliant light. When removed from ultraviolet light, their red colour does not look so beautiful. This marvellous nature of the gem-stone is called fluorescence. In it the crystal acts as a converter of wavelengths. The invisible rays of short wavelength are absorbed, and in their place is the emission of long rays equal in wavelength to red light. Those women who are eager to show off the great shine of their rubies should keep themselves in light of short wavelength. The wonderful miracle of colours can take place by applying a little oil on the hands and face and wearing garments dyed in colours.

In ultraviolet light, suddenly strange colour-changes are seen in various kinds of gems. In many rubies only a lustreless red glow is found, while in others a yellow shine appears. The artificial red stones do not change colour. Two things that distinguish artificial and real rubies —

**[p. 45]**

the silk-like glow and asterism (star-like radiance). These are present in natural gems, but in artificial ones they are wholly absent.

There is also a difference in the colour of rubies obtained from various places. The colour of rubies from Burma is less deep than that of Siamese rubies. The colour of rubies from Sri Lanka has some yellowness. The very best grade of rubies is obtained from the place named Mogok in northern Burma. In Siam, rubies are found at a place called Chantabun near Bangkok. There they are obtained in sandy soil. The ruby mines of Burma are considered the oldest, and the best and largest rubies of the world have been obtained from there. The very finest gems from the mines there, weighing thirty-two and thirty-eight carats, were sold respectively for ten thousand and twenty thousand pounds. In Burma both ruby and sapphire are found, and the main areas there in northern Burma are Mogok, Kyatpyin, Khabin, Bernath, Diniyoe, etc.

**Special gems obtained from Burma.** In 1875 the king of Burma sold off two fine rubies. Of these, one which was of fine red colour was of two carats; the other was of 4 carats. Both were cut in London. The weight of the first gem decreased to 32.3 carats and of the second to 38.6 carats [sic]. They were sold respectively for 10,900 and 20,000 pounds. In Burma a huge ruby of 400 carats was also obtained. Breaking it, three gems were made. One piece of it was sold in Calcutta for seven lakh rupees. A beautiful ruby, which was given the name "Nawab Boh," was 44 carats in its original form. After being cut it remained 20 carats. During the time of the control of Ruby Mines Limited, at one time a beautiful ruby in rough form was of 18.5 carats. This gem was perfectly clear and of a very splendid colour. After cutting it was sold for 700 pounds. Later its value was estimated at 10,000 pounds. In 1880 a ruby of 77 carats was obtained. It was sold in India in 1904 for four lakh rupees. In 1887

**[p. 46]**

a ruby of 49 carats was obtained. In 1890 a very large ruby of 204 carats was also found. In 1777 Gustavus III presented to the Czarina Catherine of Russia, on her arrival at Saint Petersburg, a ruby as large as a pigeon's egg. It was included among the gems of the Russian royal family.

Good rubies, which are mostly of deep colour, are found in considerable quantity in Thailand near Bangkok. Here rubies are found in association with red spinel stones. Rubies are also found in the mines near Kabul and to the north of the lapis lazuli mines of Badakhshan. Some rubies obtained in the state of North Carolina in America are more beautiful than the gem-stones of Burma. Rubies are also obtained in Sri Lanka, but very few. There, sapphires are in abundance.

The largest ruby obtained in Sri Lanka was of 425 carats, but in it there was certainly a blue tint. In Australia there are mines of all kinds of corundum, in which rubies are also found. In the diamond region of Southern Rhodesia, near the Somabulo forest region, some small red stones have been obtained in gem-bearing gravel along with other corundum-family gems.

The price of ruby gems is high. After cutting, their value is from 500 to 7500 rupees per carat. Rubies of the very best grade, with clarity, are sold at even higher prices. Apart from gems, ruby and sapphire are used as jewels in watches and as bearings in many scientific instruments. The red and blue stones obtained in Sri Lanka and Australia are mostly used for this work.

**Difference in rubies obtained from various places.** According to Ratna Prakash, the ruby of Burma is considered the best. Rubies obtained from there are of colour from rose-petal to deep red. If the brightest-coloured ruby from Thailand is placed before Burma's, it will remain dark-lustred. The milkiness of their ruby is washed-out. Because of this lack of milkiness, no star appears in it, whereas the milkiness of a ruby of another mine is seen collected at one place and appears moving when viewed by turning it. In Sri Lanka, at a place called Ratnapura,

**[p. 47]**

although the rubies found have more water (clarity), compared to Burma there is less elasticity. As we said before, most rubies obtained there are violet-blue tinted. In the Kabul ruby the water is thick and there is also crumbliness. The white sting (fault) is more. But the colour is beautiful. Sometimes a watery piece is also obtained which is even better than Burma's. In South India, at a place called Kagayim, a ruby mine has also come out. The gem-stone there is gummala (garnet-like) and dark blue-tinted. There is dullness in it. It is somewhat soft. Some piece is very good. On cutting it, water becomes visible in the pieces. The gem-stones obtained from there are of three kinds: gummy, transparent and semi-transparent. The ruby of Tanganyika Africa is very crumbly. In the red colour there is dark lustre, and in it a yellow tint is also mixed.

**Qualities of a superior and excellent ruby**

(1) A ruby from which, as soon as it is placed before the sun in the morning, red rays begin to scatter on all sides, is considered of great excellent quality.

(2) If on putting the ruby in milk a hundred times, the milk appears red, or red rays begin to appear, then that ruby is excellent.

(3) If on placing the ruby in darkness it shines like the sun's lustre, it will be a superior ruby.

(4) If the ruby begins to shine on being placed in lotus petals, it should be considered superior.

(5) In the morning place the ruby on a mirror before the sun. If rays are seen in the shadow part below the mirror, it is an excellent ruby.

**[p. 48]**

(6) If on rubbing the ruby on a stone the stone is worn away but the ruby is not, and its weight does not decrease, and from rubbing its beauty increases further, then that ruby should be considered of the best quality.

(7) A superior ruby is red like the petals of a red lotus, clear, sparkling, beautiful to look at, round, of even parts and limbs, and luminous.

**Testing and examination**

**Defects of ruby:** (1) sunn (without lustre), (2) dudhak (milky), (3) duranga (two colours in one and the same gem), (4) crumbliness (that easily crumbles), (5) jathara (thick water — lack of transparency), (6) jala (web), (7) par (feather-like), (8) chir (a crack), (9) dullness in colour, (10) smoky colour, (11) black or white, (12) honey colour or flecks of this colour, (13) blueness, and (14) a pit.

**Varieties of the rough of ruby:** (1) **Nima** — this appellation is given to that material which has yellowness, is defective and has thick water. (2) **Jatband** — a ruby is called this when there is clarity in its water, the colour is clean, and the colour is like a Kandhari pomegranate or red lotus or parrot's beak, or like a rose petal. (3) **Nima-jatband** — there is blueness, the water is somewhat thick and smoothness is more. (4) **Ledi-coloured ruby** — this is considered the best colour of ruby. It is like a red pomegranate with streaks.

**Identification of a genuine ruby.** (1) Placing a genuine ruby on the eyes gives a feeling of coolness. Glass or imitations soon feel warm. (2) The specific gravity (density of weight) of a genuine ruby is more than that of an artificial one. (3) If bubbles are seen or the gem is light, it should be understood to be glass or an imitation. (4) If a ruby has milkiness, there is blueness in that milkiness and the milkiness is not moving, then it is an imitation. (5) If there is a crack, it is necessary to see whether the crack is of glass or of ruby. If the crack is of glass, it will be like glass

**[p. 49]**

and there will be shine inside the crack. The crack that occurs naturally in a gem is entirely different from the crack of glass. There is no shine in it. In that crack there is no unnaturalness, rather it is crooked and there is no cleanliness in it. In an imitation there is a straight and clean crack. (6) In glass and imitation, the dots are round, rough and white-coloured and somewhere yellow. In ruby they are of that very colour, but not round. Such dots are found less in a genuine gem. If they occur, on examining minutely the difference is known. (7) There is much difference in the layers that occur in ruby and imitation. The layer of ruby is straight and that of imitation is semi-circular. This is a very important difference. (8) In the imitation gem there is also pulp and fibre. It is rough and white. The pulp of ruby is smooth and coloured. If in the imitation the part of the lap of the rough has gone into the gem, then in it white-white particles are clearly visible.

**The new method of examination.** There is an institute of natural science in Philadelphia. Its chief, Mr. Samuel G. Gordon, has discovered a new and very simple method for the examination of ruby. It is this: if there is suspicion of impurity in any ruby, place it in front of a piece of ice. If the ruby is genuine, there will be a loud sound. If it is fake, there will be no sound.

**The difference between synthetic and imitation.** In the language of jewellers, a synthetic gem is called "chhadam." In a synthetic there are defects like those of ruby, but in the milkiness there is no milk-whiteness but blueness, and it is not moving. When ultraviolet rays fall on it, both imitation and synthetic appear of orange colour, and under a microscope in both alike defects will appear.

**[p. 50]**

**Ruby and the Brihat Samhita**

According to the Brihat Samhita, from the three kinds of stones — saugandhika, kuruvinda and sphatika (rock crystal) — padmaraga (red) is born. The padmaragas born from saugandhika stone are lustrous like a red bee, the collyrium-cloud, and the jamun fruit. The padmaragas born from kuruvinda stone are of many colours, lustrous and pure. A padmaraga that is oily, shining with its own lustre, of good colour, is excellent. A padmaraga that is unclean, of dim lustre, pervaded with lines, endowed with clay etc. metals, broken, unfit to be set, and gritty is not pleasing — these are its defects. A gem that is of the colour of a bee and a peacock's neck, luminous like the flame of a lamp, which is born in the head of a serpent, is priceless. The king who wears that gem never suffers from poison or disease-caused defects. By the influence of that gem the gods ever cause rain in his kingdom, and his enemies too are destroyed.

**Various names.** Sanskrit: mukta, mauktika, shuktija, indu-ratna. Urdu-Persian: muravavid. English: pearl.

**Physical properties.** Pearl is not a mineral; it is a biological formation. Its hardness is 3.5, specific gravity from 2.65 or 2.69 to 2.84 and 2.89.

Pearl has been counted among the precious gems from time immemorial. In India, according to ancient belief, pearls are of eight kinds according to their origin:

(1) **Abhramukta** — born from the clouds. Its colour has a yellowish lustre.

(2) **Sarpamukta** — found in the hood of a serpent. It has a bluish lustre and is very shining.

**[p. 52]**

(3) **Baans-mukta** — comes out of the bamboo. Somewhat greenish-lustred, of berry shape, somewhat long.

(4) **Shukar-mukta** — comes from the head of a boar. It is like mustard oil with a little yellowness.

(5) **Gaj-mukta** — born from the temple (forehead) of an elephant of Airavata lineage. It is of the shape of a plum. There is no shine in it.

(6) **Shankh-mukta** — this is of light ochre or white colour, with very little shine, but is very smooth.

(7) **Meen-mukta** — born in the womb of a fish; with a little greenness, of the colour of the motia flower or of the pandu (yellowish-white) colour. It is said that if one keeps this pearl in the mouth and enters water, all the objects within become visible.

(8) **Shukti-mukta** — comes out from inside the oyster shell. Nowadays only the pearls obtained from the womb of the oyster are in use, and the pearls described in the other divisions above are unattainable. Scientists are not even prepared to accept that pearls can be obtained in any way other than from the oyster.

As we have said above, the present-day belief is that pearl can be obtained only from the oyster. Although it is of biological origin, its composition is especially like that of a mineral substance. Therefore we cannot call pearl a gem-stone, but it is certainly a valuable gem.

The birth of the pearl takes place in an extremely entertaining manner, and the tiny creature that gives it birth is one that makes its home inside the oyster shell. The pearl-making snail is an aquatic creature of the "mussel" species, resembling in form the scallop. In its body there are some organs like thick threads by which it can attach itself to rocks or other aquatic places. There are about two seasons in a year for its reproduction,

**[p. 53]**

when countless snails are born in the water. Those snails that obtain the support of rocks are alone fit that pearls may be formed in them.

The snail lives within two flesh coverings called the "mantle," and this mantle is filled with extremely fine cells which have the capacity to secrete a mixture of lime carbonate and other hard substances. This mixture, congealing, slowly takes the form of the shell. In every snail the shells are in two pieces of one and the same size, length, breadth and roundness. They are joined on one side and, closing well over the snail, protect it. Opening these as required, the snail obtains its food.

There are four layers in the shell. The first layer is of a substance like horn, which is called "conchiolin." This substance comes out from the mantle. The second layer is the "prismatic" layer, made of calcite or aragonite and conchiolin, and it accumulates on the surface of the shell making equal angles. By the conchiolin a frame is formed on which calcium carbonate gets deposited. This substance too is obtained from the mantle. The third layer is of nacre (mother-of-pearl), which is in reality calcium carbonate secreted from the whole surface of the mantle. Its edges are crooked and open, which gives it a beautiful shine. The fourth layer, which is called "hypostracum," lies between the snail's muscles and the surface of the shell. This layer is of plates of calcium carbonate and is at right angles to the surface of the shell.

These layers form very slowly, and from the reflection of light falling on the various layers there comes into the pearl that beautiful lustre which is called the "oriental lustre."

When the snail has built its home in the manner described above, it begins to consider itself safe; but nature has to give the world a gem like the pearl, and therefore instruments are made by which obstruction arises in the snail's security. It happens that some foreign substance — a grain of sand, an insect, or a particle of rock — enters the snail's fort.

**[p. 54]**

The snail makes every effort to get rid of it, and sometimes succeeds in expelling it. When this is not possible, the snail begins to cover that foreign substance with the substance present in the shell, and in this way layer after layer is laid over it. That substance is the very one of which the shell is made. In the end these layers take the form of a lustrous pearl. This pearl, in the end, enhances the beauty of the jeweller's box of gems. Because it is formed in this way in any form, since molecular rules are not finally obeyed in it, the shape of the pearl may be of any kind. Similarly pearls of various shapes are found — they are round, oval, pear-shaped, or of irregular form. A perfectly round pearl is considered very good and valuable.

Sometimes this foreign substance reaches between the shell and the mantle, or a boring insect bores a hole in the shell. In such a condition the pearls that form are attached to the shell, and they are cut away from the shell. They are flat and of irregular shape. These pearls are called "blister pearls," and their value is much less in comparison with round pearls.

The number of layers laid one upon another in a full pearl is impossible to know. How thin these layers are can be tested by placing the pearl's surface under a microscope. Its surface is surrounded by very fine, irregular but roughly parallel lines. These lines are in reality the edges of the fine layers of deposit. On the combined effect of the superimposition of these thin translucent plates and their adjacent interlocking ends, the quality of the pearl depends. On examining even the most beautiful artificial pearl it is found that this specific structure is wholly absent in it. The closer the ends of the serrated plates, the better the nacreous lustre.

The hardness of pearls differs from one another. Similarly there is a difference in their specific gravity. Different kinds of pearls are obtained from different seas. Merely by looking at the pearl's lustre, a skilled jeweller

**[p. 55]**

can tell which is the pearl's place of origin. Pearls are of several colours — white, black, yellow, blue, pink, red, brown, and green bearing a dark lustre. The pearls on which a light pink lustre appears are considered the best. Pearls of this kind are obtained only from the Persian Gulf.

Pearls are not cut like gem-stones. Nor are they polished. Yes, they are sometimes tinted, but mostly, for use in ornaments, only drilling is done. Pearls are used in rings, brooches, earrings, coat-buttons and other ornaments together with other gems. But pearls strung in necklaces look very beautiful, and it is in this form that their highest prices are obtained. Regarding the use of pearls, the following chaupais of the time of Shri Ramachandra ji's wedding are extremely apt:

"Then calling the wise ones, he gathered many, skilled and clever in the work of making the canopy.
Bowing to the rites they began the work, and made pillars of gold and banana trees.
Of emeralds were the leaves and fruits, of padmaraga (ruby) the flowers;
Seeing the wondrous work, even Brahma the creator was amazed.
Of green gems were all the reeds made, straight and branching, the leaves not to be discerned.
Of gold was made the creeper-work; the beautiful leaves could not be perceived.
Then weaving and binding it they made the garland-work, and here and there pearl-strings of beauty.
Of ruby, emerald, diamond and chrysolite, cutting and piercing, they made the lotuses."

But beware! Pearls are softer in comparison with other gems, so they are easily scratched. They can also break. Besides this, there is also a chemical reaction of acids upon them, and from the body's sweat too there is a likelihood of their going bad. Even when kept with great care, on account of the decay of the organic part, after some time pearls become useless. Pearls found in old burial places crumble to dust at the mere touch. The pearls set in ancient rings are not seen today.

**Places of origin.** The chief places of origin of pearl are: the Persian (now Iranian) Gulf, the Gulf of Mannar (Sri Lanka), the Australian coasts, Venezuela, Japan, the Red Sea, Panama and the islands of the Pacific Ocean. The pearls of the very best kind come from the Persian Gulf, where the whole work of pearl production is in the hands of merchants. The pearls obtained there are mostly brought to Bombay, where they are placed in different classes, drilled and coloured. Then they are sold. It also happens that pearls are sent directly to London or Paris for sale.

The coast of Sri Lanka does not hold a very important place in the production of pearls. The pearls here are small, but they are of the best kind. Here the work of removing the pearls is under government control.

On the coast of Australia work is done by modern methods, and the divers are given suitable clothing and training in the work. The pearls obtained from there have a white lustre, which is much valued in America and England. Although pearls as valuable as those of the Persian Gulf are not obtained here, yet in the year 1911 a pearl of 178 grains was found which was sold for three thousand pounds.

The pearls of Venezuela are mostly of black colour. Some pearls have a dark lustre along with a green lustre. Such pearls are rare and are considered extremely valuable. From the shellfish of the Gulf of Mexico too, beautiful black pearls are obtained.

**Pearls of different places and their specialities.** (1) **Persian Gulf** — its pearls are called "pearls of Basra." This is considered of the best kind because it is the most durable and has good shine and skin.

**[p. 57]**

(2) **Ceylon island (Sri Lanka)** — these are called "pearls of Kahil." They are very shining. In comparison with the pearls of Basra they appear dark-lustred. In weight too they are somewhat lighter than Basra's. (3) **Venezuela (South America)** — these too are like Basra's, but their shape is less round and more white. (4) **Mexico** — its Kagavasi (black-coloured) pearl is very good. (5) **China Gulf** — this pearl is of pink colour, more round in shape, softer in colour than Basra. On being worn it becomes white on account of the body's sweat and its beauty increases; but because of lack of uniformity in its colour, it is made white by chemicals. On this account its beauty decreases and some dryness also comes in it. With the least carelessness in washing, the pearl is destroyed. (6) **Darbhanga (Mussahari)** — the pearl here is much like that of the China Gulf, but it is of an inferior kind, and is mostly used for eating. A pearl of this kind is also obtained in the Gomati river. (7) **Udhan-murgi (Chanvalia)** — this, bearing rawness, is light and lustreless. Its shape is good, but by mere rubbing on cloth its weight decreases. Hence it is considered an inferior kind. (8) In South India, in the Madras province (Tamil Nadu) too, pearl is found. It resembles that of Basra. It is current under the name of the pearl of Tuticorin. (9) The pearl of Australia is white, but it is very hard. In it the marks of the shape of teeth are more numerous. Even being white, there is no beauty in it.

**South India — an ancient region of pearl production.** Writers of this country and foreign lands have written about the presence of pearl and oyster regions on the seashore of Tamil Nadu. In the Peripulus text, and Pliny and Ptolemy, mention has been made of the stores of the Korkai gulf and pearl regions. This very Korkai is the Gulf of Mannar.

The pearls removed in South India were among the chief articles of trade. From the recent excavation at Arikamedu, two miles south of Pondicherry on the eastern edge of India, it has come to light that India had a very

**[p. 58]**

advanced trade relation with Rome. Pearls were exported to Egypt, Rome, Greece and China. Several Roman writers have described the extensive use of pearls in the Roman empire.

The name of Tuticorin has from very early times been famous for pearl regions. In 1630 a missionary bishop named Father Jordanus came to India. He wrote that in the pearl regions of Tuticorin and Sri Lanka about 8,000 boats were in extensive use.

The waters of Tuticorin are among the world's three chief regions where oysters are found on a commercial scale. The other regions are the Persian Gulf, Basra, and the edge of the ocean adjoining Japan. South India is truly fortunate that it can gather even the treasure of the sea.

**Testing and examination.**

**Defects of pearl:** (1) **Garaj** — that is, cracking in the skin. (2) **Lahar** (a fine line). (3) **Gidli** — this is like a joint in the pearl, as if two pieces have been joined together. Around such a defective pearl there is a circular line. (4) **Massā** — like a wart. If it is of dark colour it is considered inauspicious. (5) Rough, lustreless. (6) **Chobha** — like the mark of smallpox. (7) Blister. (8) Chhavva. (9) **Matia** — in whose interior there is clay. (10) **Myani** — a pearl having this defect is like wood in the middle. Above, the pearl's skin is like the alilli (a kind of shell). The wood in its middle is clearly visible. At the time of drilling, besides the skin the wood also is easily drilled. There is not the hardness as in other pearls. On the alilli a dark lustre also appears.

**Historical pearls.** In the year 1901 a pearl was found in the Persian Gulf which was of 28 grains. It was sold in Australia for 3,000 pounds. The largest pearl in the world is in the museum of Henry Philip Hope. Its length is 2 inches, its thickness 3.5 inches and its circumference 4.9 inches. Three-quarters of its colour is white and one quarter the colour of the cassis. Its value is 12,000 pounds and its weight 454 carats. A pearl of 30 carats is in the royal crown of Australia; another is in the crown of the Shah of Iran, which is of pendant shape. An excellent pearl is in the museum of Zosima in Moscow; its name is "La Peregrina." There is yet another famous group of pearls called the "Great Southern Cross," in which nine large pearls are joined together in the form of a cross, that is, a swastika. It was found in 1886 in an oyster caught in a net off the coast of Western Australia. In the gem collection of London there are several gems of strange form.

**[p. 59]**

A collection of excellent pearls was with the former Maharaja Gaikwad. It was in the form of seven pearl necklaces. Its value was estimated at about one crore rupees.

The Maharaja Gaikwad also had another pearl named "Peregrine," whose value is about two and a half crore rupees.

**Cultured pearls.** In 1921, when cultured pearls first appeared in the jewellers' markets of London and Paris, people were struck with wonder, because between them and real pearls the least difference did not appear. But soon the jewellers came to know the difference, and cultured pearls came into the category of other synthetic gems.

**How the cultured pearl is made.** A half-to-three-quarters pearl is in a way a "blister" pearl. An artificial stimulating substance is introduced between the shell and the mantle. This substance is a small bead of nacre. Then the snail begins to lay layers on this very bead, just as it does when a natural foreign substance enters. The "blister" pearl formed in this way is cut from the shell, and over it is laid a covering of nacre. Then it is polished, but the joining of the nacre and the "blister" pearl can be easily seen. These pearls are so set in rings etc. that the joint remains hidden within.

**Imitation pearls.** Fake pearls, which are also called "imitation or momiya-mati" (wax pearls), are hollow spheres of thin glass. They are covered with a substance prepared from fish-scales, from which the pearl-lustre comes into them. Then the glass sphere is filled with wax. Transparent white glass is used in imitating a natural gem. These fake pearls can be easily recognised on close examination. In any condition these fine serrated marks are not found on them — they are the speciality only of true and cultured pearls. By their presence it can be known whether the pearl developed inside the shell or not.

When fake, that is, imitation, pearls are placed between the teeth, they feel smooth; on the contrary, true pearls feel gritty. The simplest and best test is to examine the ends of the drilled hole in them with the help of a lens. By doing so it is known that they are made of a glass bead, and if the pearls are of any substance other than glass, then marks of some of the covering coming off will certainly be found on them.

In imitation pearls the inner or outer surface of the glass sphere is covered with the substance made from fish-scales. After determining the specific gravity of the greatest number of pearls of both these kinds, Robert Webster stated that the specific gravity of solid imitation pearls is considerably higher than that of true pearls. It is from 2.88 to 3.18.

It has also been observed that shining beads of hematite are sold in the form of pearls, but this is a deception. In it there is a blazing metallic lustre, and its specific gravity is very high, that is, 5.0.

Many optical and X-ray-related methods of testing pearls have been discovered, but here a simple method is being mentioned. The basis of this method is the difference in average density between pearls that appear true, like cultured ones.

**[p. 61]**

In it the heavy liquid bromoform is used. If this test has to be kept limited to a single liquid, it is necessary to lighten bromoform by mixing in benzene, toluene or monobromo-naphthalene, so much so that in it a piece of Iceland spar, that is, calcite, used as an indicator, slowly rises to the surface. The specific gravity of pure calcite is 2.71; therefore, after lightening in this way, the density of the liquid is taken as 2.715 or 2.72. In such a liquid very many true pearls float. This happens with about fifty per cent of true pearls. If a true pearl does sink in the liquid, it does so slowly. On the contrary, most of the cultured pearls sink in the liquid, and some of them certainly settle to the bottom immediately.

**Natural pearls.** This pearl too is made like the cultured one by entering the oyster's womb by a machine, but in it the inserted chemical comes out in the drilling. Therefore, on the machine it appears real. Although a natural pearl appears real by machine, yet there is so much difference in its parts and colour that it can be recognised. On it a blue lustre clearly glimmers.

**The five methods of testing pearls.** (1) In a cultured pearl there is a bead of glass-like foreign substance, which, on being drilled, is seen in the hole. (2) There is no uniformity in the hole. The hole of the cultured pearl is wider in the middle. On drilling, either the bead comes out or, if it remains, the place in the middle becomes larger than the place of the hole's beginning, and inside this bead keeps shining. This can be seen by an instrument. (3) In the cultured pearl the upper skin is somewhat hard, whereas in the true pearl's skin there is softness. (4) If real, cultured and fake pearls are kept in cow's urine or khara (alkaline matter) for twenty-four hours, the real one will not be spoilt; in the others a difference will appear. (5) On rubbing the pearl in rice, the shine of the true pearl will increase and of the fake will decrease.

**[p. 62]**

**Names of the shapes of pearls:** (1) Kilki, (2) Sira, (3) Chokha-sira, (4) Sujani, (5) Tir-gama, (6) Gama, (7) Khadi-kamar, (8) Kamar, (9) Batla, (10) Simta-ansar, (11) Chikna-ansar, (12) Karkar, (13) Paya, (14) Taliya, (15) Baithki.

When pearls are worn for a long time, from the effect of dust, water and sweat their lustre goes away. In ancient times many methods of polishing them were current. It is said that a dirty pearl used to be fed to a pigeon and kept in its belly for twenty hours. After taking it out (which could probably be done only by killing the pigeon) shine would come into the pearl, although it would become lighter in weight. Another method is told: rice and water were put in a vessel and heated from below. When the water became lukewarm, taking it off the fire, the pearl was cleaned for some time with the rice-water starch. By this the pearl became clean and shine came into it.

**Mukta-shodhana (cleansing of pearls).** For cleaning the dirt of an old pearl, in ancient times it was also washed with the water of soapnut (aritha), or a hollow was made in a radish, the pearl was filled into it, and the remaining part of the hollow was filled with raw sugar; then the pearl became clean. By this operation the pearl became clean and was not spoilt either. In other operations, with a little carelessness the pearl is spoilt. By the modern process, old pearls are cleaned with hydrogen peroxide and ether. In two hours this process makes the pearl clean and shining, but with carelessness it becomes dry. Before putting it in the chemical it is proper to see whether the pearl is even fit to be put in it. After putting it in the chemical too, attention should constantly be kept that the pearl may not become dry.

**Pearl is not sold by the ratti-weight.** Unlike other gems, pearl is not sold by the weight of rattis. It is sold by the "chav." Chav is a name of a weight whose method of calculation is this: whatever the pearl is in rattis, multiply it by that same number and make its square. Then, taking out three-fourths of it, take out three-fourths of that part too. Whatever answer comes, that many pieces are added to the above square; the number that emerges is the pearl's chav. For example, let us take a pearl of four rattis. Multiplying it by four, 16 comes. Its three-fourths part is 12. Three-fourths of 12 is 9. Now the pearl's chav comes to 9 pieces and 16. If the pearl is more than one, then dividing by the number of pearls in those chavs, whatever quotient comes, that is the chav of those pearls. Its table is like this:

- 16 badam = 1 piece
- 54 pieces = 1 ana
- 100 pieces = 1 chav

**Pearl dust — a splendid face powder.** There is an entertaining story of the coming to know of this distinction. A French traveller once in Lanka, seeing the shining faces of Malayali girls, was struck with wonder. Their faces were of dark colour, but upon them there was an extremely attractive glitter. Later, on enquiry, it was learnt that those girls used to do the cutting and trimming of pearls. The dust of the drilled pearls was the stimulant of their body's lustre. After that, in France the dust of pearls began to be sold in the form of a beautifying powder.

**Various names.** Sanskrit: pravala, vidruma, latamani, angarakamani, raktanga. Hindi: munga. Urdu-Persian: mirjan. English: coral.

Coral has been used in ornaments from ancient times. The Romans used it greatly and also made amulets of it. There are many beliefs regarding its divine power, the account of which we shall give in a separate chapter. In India too, from ancient times, coral has been an accepted gem. On account of its attractive lustre and colour, coral has been included among the Navaratnas, although it is not a valuable gem.

Like pearl, coral too is not a mineral substance. Its origin is considered vegetal. But it is obtained, like pearl, only from the sea. In appearance its form looks like the branches of a creeper. According to modern scientists, this coral is a hard deposit (like coral-skeleton) of an aquatic animal, and its greater part is made of calcium carbonate coming from the sea. A creature of the name of coral, which is slippery like pulp or a leech, attaches itself to certain rocks submerged in the sea. In suitable conditions it collects on its upper and lower part hard deposits of calcium carbonate. In structure, red coral is like those little tubes which are joined to one another and then grow on like a tree. The coral-making deposits are not found in all seas or in all parts of any sea. For them a certain temperature is necessary. The coral deposits have been found at a depth of about nine hundred feet, but the greater the depth of the place of origin, the less the depth of the coral's colour. Since coral is made by an aquatic creature, several years are taken for the deposit to mature, and until the deposit becomes mature it is not touched.

The coral aquatic creature, which makes a tree without leaves but with branches, is sometimes like the human body, but ordinarily it is about a foot high and an inch thick. It has an extremely beautiful red colour, and it can be polished well. In it there are chambers like honeycombs — in these chambers the creatures live. Over the stem a net-like alilli is spread. Looking with a microscope at the insects sitting in these their homes, it is found that on their mouths there is a pointed moustache. In them the sense of touch is extremely powerful — by this very power they obtain their food. Their food is small sea-worms or small particles of plants.

These creatures make their creeper-like covering on rocks submerged in the sea (as we have said above), that is, they make coral, which is their home for living. The pearl-making snail makes the pearl within its home, but the coral deposit is in a way the insect's home itself.

A person named Ligio Nice has, while describing coral in the year 1502, told that coral grows in the sea like a tree. There are no leaves in it. According to him, in size it does not grow bigger than two feet.

**[p. 66]**

The kind of coral that is used in ornaments is called "Corallium rubrum." Corals are of red colours, but there is a predominance of various red shades. Red corals are also found in greater number, although corals of white and yellow colours also occur. There is more demand for corals of pink, red and deep red colours. Coral is often also coloured to improve its appearance, but these colours are not durable. Although the natural colour of corals comes by itself, it cannot be cut with certainty; but the guess is that they get their colour from a mixture of calcium and magnesium carbonate and iron oxide.

**Places of origin.** On the shores of the Mediterranean Sea are the chief places where coral creatures are found. Coral is obtained from the coasts of Algeria, Tunisia, Corsica, Sardinia and Sicily. On the coasts of the Japanese islands too, coral deposits are found, but the work of removing coral is done mostly by the Italians, and they are given the form of gems in the city of Naples. Mostly coral is of the colour of the blood of the vine (morone), which is also called "Morello coral." Some coral has also been obtained from the shore of the Adriatic Sea. Coral is also obtained from the coast of Spain, which is of deep red colour. In former times the French, the Spanish and the English too used to take out corals from the sea-coasts, and at that time Marseille was the field of this industry; but now the chief field of this industry is only Naples, where thousands of persons remain engaged in this work.

At one time blue-coloured coral was obtained on the coast of Africa, but now coral of this colour is not seen.

**Physical properties.** Corals are also semi-transparent, but mostly they are opaque. Coral is found in white, pink, orange, red and black colours. Black coral differs from the corals of the other colours in this respect, that it is not made mostly of calcium carbonate, but of a horn-like substance. The properties of limy coral are just like those of calcite (churnashma). A special characteristic of coral is that on the surface of whatever rock in the sea it is attached, it always stands in a long form. Its fibres, from the centre of each branch, are spread on the surface at right angles to its length. On viewing with a microscope, each branch appears like a streak parallel to its length. Coral appears dull at the place of fracture. The specific gravity of coral is 2.65. Its hardness is from 3.5 to 4. Its refractive indices are 1.486 and 1.658;

**[p. 67]**

in this way the double refraction is high. The refractive indices of black coral are 1.56 and 1.57, and the double refraction is about .1; its relative density is 1.37 and hardness 3. When hydrochloric acid is poured on coral, it effervesces. On black coral its effect does not take place. If black coral is touched with a heated wire, such a foul smell comes from it as comes from burning hair.

**Testing and examination.**

**Excellent coral.** According to Ayurveda, a coral endowed with seven kinds of specialities is considered auspicious: (1) like a ripe bimba fruit, (2) round, (3) long, (4) straight, (5) smooth, (6) free from wounds like notch, cavity or protuberance, and (7) thick. Its colour resembles sindur, hingula or singarf.

White-coloured coral is also beautiful and lustrous. In Bengal, white coral is much liked. The real fact is this, that however unlike other gems it may be, its attraction is on account of its beautiful colour. For this very reason it is one of the Navaratnas.

**Defects of coral:** stain, white specks, boring, tear, two-colouredness, and a pit.

**Imitation coral.** Imitation coral is heavier than genuine coral. On viewing it with a lens, fine and coarse grains are clearly seen, which are like melted glass. On rubbing imitation coral, a clear sound like glass comes from it.

**Historical coral.** The custom of beautiful carving on coral is now no more, but some carved corals of ancient times still exist. In an exhibition held in Berlin in 1880, a coral necklace worth 600 pounds was displayed. With the royal family of Italy there was a carved work hilt whose value was 36 pounds.

**The use of coral in ornaments.** Coral is used in rings, earrings and necklaces. There is also the custom of garlands of coral for worship;

**[p. 68]**

but now, their price having decreased, its use has lessened. There is great currency of wearing necklaces made of small coral beads together with beads of pearls of the same size.
## Chapter 6 · Navaratna: Emerald (Panna)

> **Printed pages:** 69–74
>

> - The family of beryl

> - Places of origin and differences by place

**Various names.** Sanskrit: marakata, pachi, garutmat, haritamani, garudankit, garudodgirna, garalari, sauparni. Hindi: panna. Urdu-Persian: zamarrad. English: emerald.

**Physical properties.** Hardness 7.75; specific gravity from 2.69 to 2.80; refractive index 1.57–1.58. Regular six-sided form. Double refraction. Dispersion 0.01 (not much). Transparent or translucent. Dichroism — green and bluish-green.

The emerald of the beryl class, with its green velvet-like, incomparable, captivating colour, is counted among the world's precious gems, and sometimes a flawless gem is of several times more value than a diamond. In composition it is a silicate of aluminium and beryllium. Aquamarine, golden beryl and the red gem-stone morganite are also of the beryl family, and their chemical composition is like that of emerald. In emerald there is also 1.2 per cent water.

**[p. 70]**

To obtain a flawless emerald of more than five or six carats is, if not impossible, certainly difficult. If it does come to hand, its value will be extreme. Hence emerald-lovers have to be contented with small gems alone. If an emerald is not even large, yet if it is flawless and attractively cut and set, it irresistibly draws the mind towards itself. But emerald keeps its personality very much intact. Even if emeralds are set amid diamonds, they keep their attractive personality separate and do not hide from the eye. If in any ornament emeralds and emeralds alone are set, it seems as if their green lustre were laughing aloud.

The presence of a flaw in emerald is inevitable. In the selection of an emerald, this alone is to be seen — in which gem there are the fewest flaws, because in ruby and sapphire their deep colour, after cutting, usually throws a veil over their flaws; but in the case of emerald this does not happen.

Even a flawed emerald of good appearance defeats other valuable gems. This is the very reason that the emerald, giving coolness to the eyes, is so popular.

The gem-stones of emerald are mostly found in the form of hexagonal prisms. It is an important fact worth knowing that emeralds are obtained mostly flawed. On the contrary, the other stones of the beryl class, aquamarine (green sapphire), are found in large pieces. This is the reason that flawless emeralds are very precious. As we have said, in most emeralds flaws and foreign substances are mixed, on account of which the value of these gem-substances decreases. Those emeralds which bear a yellow lustre are not valuable. The best colours of emerald are considered to be deep velvety and grass-green. The glassy gleam of an excellent stone gives it a beautiful velvety appearance.

In emerald there is less cleavage, and for such a valuable stone its hardness is also low, because its specific gravity is also low. Hence it is also less in weight. At the time of setting it in ornaments, great care is necessary because emeralds are brittle. Therefore it is also necessary that its girdle should not be cut too thin. In stones of good colour, dichroism clearly appears. Emerald is mostly cut in the "step cut."

**[p. 71]**

The gems of rings, brooches etc. appear in this cut. But among Indian artisans its cabochon cut is very dear. On the contrary, aquamarine is made in the form of brilliant cut on the front side and step cut on the back.

In its original or pure form, beryl is colourless, but it is rarely without a green or blue tint. The green-coloured gem is emerald. The green colour of emerald is due to chromic oxide. If in glass also the quantity of chromic oxide present in emerald be mixed, its colour too becomes green. On heating the emerald, the water present in it flies away, but its colour remains the same. Acids too have no effect on emerald.

In some mines found in South Africa, emeralds are found together with tourmaline, crystal, apatite and feldspar. Most of the emeralds taken out from here are flawed, cracked or bearing smokiness. Sometimes flawless gems have also been obtained whose value was a hundred pounds per carat. The other places of origin of emerald are Russia and south-west Africa. The emeralds of Russia bear yellowness, and the African emeralds, deep green with some blueness, remain flawed.

In Austria, at the place of Salzburg, there is a very old emerald mine from which gems of a very common kind were obtained. Emeralds are obtained from Brazil, but they bear a yellow colour and are flawed.

Now mines of emerald have been found in India. India is the chief centre of the cutting, polishing and buying and selling of emerald.

**Difference according to place of origin.** The difference in the goods of various mines is like this: (1) **America (Colombia)** — the goods of the mines here are robust. Its emerald is famous under the name of "bux." The colour and water of the goods here are the best. In some emeralds of this mine gold-fly (a species of pyrites) is also found. In the old mine, yellowness, colour, water and elasticity are more. Most of the goods are oily. The goods of the new mine are glassy, and in it yellowness is less. (2) **Russia** — all kinds of goods are found in the mines here. But the attraction, in comparison with America, is less,

**[p. 72]**

and its hardness too is comparatively less. The water is also less than in Colombian emerald. (3) **Africa** — the goods here are mostly bearing a dark lustre and having black specks. At the time of making, its very small pieces have to be made. In practice it is also called "bottle," because its colour is like that of a bottle of mixed green and black colour. (4) **Udaipur** — the goods here are of deep colour but brittle. The water is less. (5) **Ajmer** — in the goods here there is yellowness. The colour is also attractive and the water also good, but in comparison with Colombian, the hardness is much less. In the new goods more yellowness than in Colombian goods is found. (6) **Brazil** — this emerald has begun to come into the market for some days. In it there is enough tear and yellowness in sufficient quantity. In the goods prepared from it yellowness comes in more. (7) **Pakistan** — mines of emerald have also come out here. Their emerald is of very deep colour and brittle. (8) **Sandawana** — this is in Africa. The emerald here is called Sandawana. Its goods are considered the best. In it yellowness, and colour like the old emerald, is also found. (9) **Rhodesia** — the emerald famous under the name of Rhodesia has its mines in Africa. Its goods are somewhat thick in water, and the black specks are not like the bottle's. In colour too the dark lustre is less in comparison with the bottle's. (10) From Colombia a new lot has come which is famous under the name of "Dwipechi." Its structure is of six buds, and rubbing the buds, the goods of the middle kernel are prepared. In it the other blemishes of emerald are found in very little quantity. Its inner structure is wholly different from other lots. Such a lot has appeared for the first time in the gem world. (11) **Meech** — the emerald that used to come from Meech was of very deep colour, but there was absolutely no shine in it.

**Excellent emerald.** That emerald which is of green colour, glossy, bearing smoothness, transparent and with bright rays, is considered an excellent emerald. An excellent emerald should be watery and free from blemishes.

**[p. 73]**

**Testing and examination.**

**Identification of a genuine emerald.** In India in ancient times there was imitation glass of emerald, but now scientists have brought out an imitation of emerald. Glass can be recognised very easily: (1) In glass there are white beads. (2) On fracture there are shining streaks. (3) If glass is rubbed on a stone with turmeric, the turmeric will become red. (4) If the glass is seen through the Chelsea filter (a kind of filter), the emerald will appear of pink colour and the glass's colour will remain green; but if the imitation is seen through the Chelsea filter, it will appear like emerald. (5) In the imitation there is no yellowness. (6) Fibres etc. are rough and white. (7) In the imitation, all those flaws that are in the real stone appear, but there is a difference between these flaws and the flaws of the real stone, which is known by mere looking. (8) The tear of the imitation is like the tear of glass. (9) The colour of a genuine emerald appears green even in artificial light.

**Defects of emerald:** (1) **Ganja** — thick water; (2) **Abhraki** — having a shine like mica. This is that emerald which comes out together with mica, and some part of mica enters it and it begins to shine clearly; (3) a pit; (4) a tear; (5) two-colouredness; (6) dull; (7) black or yellow speck; (8) gold-fly; (9) rough, lustreless; (10) brittle.

**Historical emeralds.** (1) **The emerald of Humayun's cup.** — It is said that the Mughal emperor Humayun had cups of emerald; they broke. Whatever pieces of them are found are called "emeralds of the cup." They are emeralds of the best kind. (2) **Jagat Seth's emerald.** — It is said that a foreign sailor came to Jagat Seth, a resident of Murshidabad,

**[p. 74]**

who had brought with him emerald rough in abundant quantity. This goods is still famous under the name of Jagat Seth's emerald. (3) **One emerald in India** — with the former Maharaja Tej Singh of Alwar, which is of 365 rattis. Its value is about thirty lakh rupees. (4) **One emerald in England** — with the Duke of Devonshire. This is the largest among the emeralds obtained so far. It is of 1347 carats. In it there are also some flaws.

**Various names.** Sanskrit: pushparaga, pita-sphatika, pitamani, jivaratna etc. Hindi: pukhraj. Persian: yaqut. English: topaz.

**Physical properties.** Hardness 8; specific gravity from 3.53 to 3.60; refractive indices 1.618 and 1.627; double refraction 0.008 and dispersion 0.014.

Topaz, or pushparaga, is the most popular yellow gem among jewels. It is used in pins, brooches or pendants, rings and ear ornaments. The relation of topaz with the yellow colour is so intimate that it is generally supposed that topaz's own real colour is yellow. The real fact is that pure topaz is a colourless gem. In former times every yellow gem used to be called topaz, and yellow olivine (chrysolite) and yellow crystal citrine were sold under the name of topaz. Even today, yellow sapphire is sold under the names of oriental topaz, smoky crystal, smoky topaz, citrine or yellow crystal, Scotch topaz etc. — but in reality these are not topaz. "Oriental

**[p. 76]**

topaz," or yellow sapphire, has hardness 9 and specific gravity 4; "Scotch topaz" has hardness 7 and specific gravity 3.65, while real topaz has hardness 8 and specific gravity about 3.53.

**Colour and colour-change.** From the point of view of chemical composition, topaz is aluminium silicate, but according to its scientific composition it should contain the element fluorine 20.6 per cent, whereas its quantity is only 15.5. On account of the quantity of water present in it, the fluorine becomes somewhat less. Besides water, some other impurities too remain present in topaz. This is the very reason that, though colourless by nature, it is available in a water-clear condition but rarely. Topazes bearing lustres from yellow to sherry-wine are more current. Topazes of brownish-grey, light blue and light green colour are not obtained. Most of the pink topazes sold in the market have been made pink by an artificial process. The Brazilian topazes, of colours from brown to yellow or deep yellow, are placed in a small crucible or hookah-bowl, and in it is filled sand or such a substance like magnesia on which no chemical process takes place by heat etc. On heating it carefully, the natural colour will come out, and on cooling, the pink colour will appear. It is also a wonderful thing that as long as the topaz remains hot the pink colour will not appear; on its becoming cool, it becomes permanent. It has also been seen that in this process, if the heat be kept slow, then instead of a pink colour, a light pink colour like the skin of a salmon fish comes. The yellow topaz obtained from Sri Lanka becomes colourless on heating. The colour of the light yellow-brown topaz obtained from Russia fades on being kept in the sunlight. This is the reason that in the British Museum the topazes obtained from Russia are kept covered.

**Auspicious topaz.** According to Indian belief, the description of an excellent and auspicious topaz is like this:

> Pushparaga is auspicious in eight ways — heavy, glossy, clean, thick, even, soft, smooth.

**[p. 77]**

An auspicious topaz has eight qualities: being placed on the hand it should be heavy; its appearance should be glossy; it should be clean, that is, there should be no spots in it; the grain should be large (thick); even, that is, free from layers; soft; having a lustre like the lustre of a fully-blown yellow Kaner flower; and on touching, appearing smooth. A topaz of the best kind, on rubbing on the touchstone, increases its colour still more.

For the unacceptable and flawed topaz it has been told that:

> A lustreless, rough, harsh, yellow-dark, uneven, bay-coloured, brown, and pallid topaz — that should be rejected.

That is: a topaz whose shine does not illuminate its colour (yellowness) is devoid of lustre; which on touching feels rough like sand; which bears dryness; in whose yellowness blackness is mixed; which has drop-drops of black colour; which is high-low (uneven); which is of the colour of a raisin; which is of a mixed red-yellow colour; which is of a yellow-white (pallid) colour — such a topaz is flawed and therefore to be rejected.

**Structure and origin.** The crystalline structure of gems is an essential scientific part of their identification. Most gems are found in the form of crystals. A crystal is called that natural solid substance which has a fixed shape, whose all surfaces are plane and smooth, and whose structure too is of a fixed form. Whatever shape appears from outside, its inner structure is of that very shape, as if it has no concern with its outer figure; and again, in whatever shape the crystal of any mineral is found at one place, the crystal of that mineral found in any corner of the world will be found in that very shape. Therefore the shape of the crystal is an essential element in identifying it or knowing its difference from other kinds of crystals. From the point of view of shape, the crystals of minerals are considered of six kinds.

Topaz and chrysolite (olivine) are gems of the orthorhombic group. In the crystals of this group, three (axes) make right angles with one another, but the axes are unequal, and the crystals are generally of prism or triangle shape.

**[p. 78]**

**Origin.** Topaz is generally found in granite, gneiss and pegmatite rocks. In these rocks, from the interaction of the water-vapour and fluorine gas coming out of the igneous substances, topaz is formed there. Along with topaz, in these very rocks there are also the minerals tourmaline, crystal, tungsten etc.; but topaz, being heavy and durable, is also found on the river in the form of pebbles that have flowed down from these rocks.

In Saxony, topaz is found deposited as a lining in crystal rocks. In Russia and Siberia, blue-coloured topaz is found in the caves of granite rocks. The topazes obtained from the mines of Brazil are of the best kind. The topazes found in the mines of Rhodesia are of the fine kind; on cutting, they look beautiful. Similarly, good topazes have also been found in Sri Lanka, Japan, Mexico, Tasmania, New England etc. The topaz of Sri Lanka is yellow, light green or colourless, and is famous under the name of "water sapphire." In the topaz of Burma there is more elasticity and smoothness.

**The polishing of topaz.** To awaken its beauty, it is cut in the "brilliant" and "step" cuts. In large pieces some new facets are also made. Its girdles are made in oval, circular or elliptical shapes.

**Easily cleaving.** While doing all this, attention should be paid to this that topaz cleaves easily. Topaz cleaves easily at right angles to the edge of the basal pinacoid. At the time of cutting and wearing the topaz, attention should be paid that it does not break on the cleavage plane. But in hardness it is less than diamond and the corundum-class stones (ruby and sapphire) alone, therefore it can be polished well. It has its own unique smooth shine. In dull topazes, dichroism appears less, but in strongly coloured topazes it is distinct. By rubbing topaz, electricity can be produced from it — this is called the pyroelectric property. In this property it is inferior among all gems only to tourmaline.

**Useful for medicine.** Taking the help of the Puranic tradition, Acharya Varahamihira, in the Brihat Samhita, also expressed this

**[p. 79]**

possibility regarding the origin of gems, that from the various limbs of the demon Bali the various gems might have originated. Even if this possibility is not accepted as a scientific fact, yet the use of gems in medicine which the experts of physiology have written certainly accords with this legend. According to Rasa Tarangini, topaz is light, of cold potency, pacifying phlegm and wind, and an antidote to poison. According to the Puranas, its origin is considered from the skin (charma) of Bali. It is an enemy of leprosy and skin diseases. It destroys the following ailments: topaz should be ground for two days in rose water and kevada water, then, pounding it in the quantity of kohl (soot), dried in the sun. The physicians use the ash (bhasma) of white topaz. Its dose is from a quarter to half a ratti. In gem-medicine too, white topaz is used. On viewing through a prism glass it appears sky-coloured. On being placed in alcohol, white topaz shines like diamond.

On rubbing or heating topaz, electric waves come out of it. On the basis of this speciality, at the time of childbirth there is mention of heating it and giving it in the hands of the pregnant woman. It is said that by this the pregnant woman gets ease in delivery.

**According to astrology, topaz is the representative of Jupiter.** Those persons whose birth-date falls in the sign of Sagittarius, or in the interval from 15 December to 14 January, their gem is topaz. A yellow topaz of seven or twelve carats should especially be worn set in a gold ring on the third finger. According to an ancient text collected in the British Museum: "One who wears topaz is not afraid at night. It is certainly intelligence-increasing; it calms anger and madness and removes the apprehension of sudden death."

**Substitute.** Those persons who cannot buy a genuine topaz may wear its sub-gem "sunela" or "sonaila." Sonela, the citrine, is a yellow stone. It looks like yellow topaz, but its shine is less and its parts are soft, and it is fully transparent.

**Genuine topaz and fake topaz.** Though everyone — jeweller and ordinary people alike — well knows topaz, yet still the mistake of taking citrine,

**[p. 80]**

rock-crystal, and fake imitation-glass topazes to be topaz is often made. In olden times, the epithet "oriental" was added before every yellow gem and it was called topaz. Nowadays jewellers sell genuine topaz under the name of precious topaz.

Though the precious topaz is famous for its transparent yellow colour, yellow-brown and orange-brown colour, topaz is also obtained with red in the middle (which usually comes only on heating), light blue, very light green, violet-light-greenish, and also colourless; but it is difficult to identify it by outward colour and form alone.

**Stones that look like topaz.** In the following gems and stones the confusion of topaz is possible: rock-crystal (called rock-crystal); tourmaline; gems of the corundum group (pink, yellow and light blue); gems of the beryl class (golden beryl, aquamarine etc.); synthetic corundum; and glass.

Among these, tourmaline and the fake topazes made of glass look in appearance like real topaz. Their refractive index is just equal to topaz's refractive index. The differences are: (1) The specific gravity of tourmaline is much less than topaz's. (2) There is no double refraction in glass. Although topaz is a gem of light lustre, yet in it pleochroism is found more than expected. In yellow topaz, clearly three colours — brownish-yellow, yellow and orange-yellow — are seen. In blue topaz, two colours — colourlessness and light blue — are seen.

For identifying it from yellow rock-crystal, its specific gravity should be found out. The simple method is this: mixing any liquid among benzene etc. in bromoform, make it of 2.65 specific gravity. In this the real topaz will sink. But colourless or yellow rock-crystal, amethyst etc., all other stones, will either hang suspended, or sink slowly, or rise slowly.

The difference of topazes of other colours from similar stones can be made by knowing their refractive index. For this the refractometer should be used. This instrument is genuine and reliable — but in gems of very high refractive index, like diamond, gomed, zircon etc., it cannot be used. If the refractive index of the sample being tested is not about 1.66, then it cannot be topaz. If its refractive index is around 1.63, it will be one of these three: (1) topaz, (2) tourmaline, (3) paste. If two edges appear and there is a difference of 0.02 in the refractive index, then the sample is tourmaline. If there is dichroism, or if the sample scratches a polished crystal or rock-crystal, then it will not be paste. If it is paste, then on viewing through a microscope, one or two bubbles or whirlpool-like line-marks will appear in it. If there is no crystal-like pointed part or cone in it, it cannot be topaz. A light red or orange gleam indicates topaz.

**Various names.** Sanskrit: vajra, pavika, vidyut, hiraka, arkabhidura, abhedya, kulisha etc. Hindi-Punjabi: heera. Urdu-Persian: almas. English: diamond.

**Physical properties.** Hardness 10; specific gravity from 3.15 to 3.53; refractive index from 2.417 to 2.46 (2.402 in red light, 2.4175 in yellow, 2.427 in green, and 2.465 in violet). In composition it is pure crystalline coal (carbon) — nothing is harder than it; after complete combustion it becomes carbon dioxide gas.

**Unconquerable and incomparable.** On account of its incomparable qualities, diamond has no equal not only among minerals, but also among gem-substances. The single unconquerable power of Indra, the king of gods, described in the Vedas, is the "vajra." It is incomparable and irrefutable. In the weapons of the commander of the "vajra" (thunderbolt) army is a group of unconquerable weapons; somewhere it is the piercing rays of the sun and somewhere the blazing flames of the light of knowledge which cut even the dense

**[p. 83]**

darkness of ignorance. Seeing these very qualities in the diamond found in mineral form, the scholars of etymology gave the diamond, in Sanskrit — the mother of languages, the divine language — the name "vajra." Then the word "diamond" too is made from the Latin word "adamas," and the meaning of "adamas" too is unconquerable.

**The king of gems.** Among mineral substances, those are called "gems" which have three special qualities — beauty, durability and rarity. Beauty: being attractive to the eye is the first quality of any gem. But the substance which, wearing away quickly and breaking, scatters, however beautiful it may be, is not worth collecting. Then even if it be beautiful and durable too, yet if it is easily available to everyone everywhere, even then there is no desire in the mind to collect it.

**Raising the status of others.** The diamond is itself great, and whatever it adorns, it makes that also more interesting and more beautiful. If an emerald or a topaz be placed on a piece of wood that has floated down in water, the emerald itself will blaze up and the topaz itself will become resplendent like the sun; but place a diamond on that piece of wood, and the wood will appear whiter, more pleasant, more crystalline and more pure. A diamond given into the dull hands of an old man gives him courage. As if the little set diamonds enjoy raising the status of every other gem set with them.

**Utterly stubborn, proud and hard.** Where the gem-faced diamond is liberal towards other gems, there it is itself great and proud. The octahedral or hexagonal rough of diamond coming out of the mine is wrapped in a covering of its own same-kind substance. To know the species of the diamond, first this covering has to be removed, and then, polishing it, it is burnished.

This glory belongs only to India, that first of all India proved that the temperament of one as hard and proud as the diamond can be corrected by polishing with its own particles. For the first time, in the thirteenth century, the Indian gem-makers alone discovered the method of making well-fashioned gems by grinding the diamond with pieces of the diamond. According to the famous French jeweller Baron Tavernier, "in India flawless diamonds were not cut — the flawed diamonds were cut and their facets were made." India should be proud of this, that when in the western countries the dawn of modern civilization had not even arisen, long before that, in India, the industry of diamonds was in existence, and such polished diamonds were exported from here. In the middle ages this industry was at its zenith in India, because the attraction of the Mughal emperors towards diamonds was very great.

**Mention in Kautilya's Arthashastra.** Chanakya has described the diamond in detail in the famous text "Arthashastra." According to the place of origin and according to their colour, an interesting description of the various names of diamonds is found in the "Arthashastra." Kautilya has told that diamonds and other chief gems are the treasury of the state, or in other words, the chief foundation of the state. He writes:

> From the mines arises the treasury; from the treasury the army (danda) is born.
> The earth is obtained by the treasury and the army; the earth adorns the treasury.

The king's treasury is filled by minerals; if there is a treasury, there will be an army, and when there is an army, by it alone will be the acquisition and protection of the kingdom.

In the Brihat Samhita it is told that vajra (diamond), indranila, marakata, karketana, lala, rudhira, vaidurya, pulaka, vimalaka, rajamanu, sphatika, chandrakanta, saugandhika, gomedaka, shankha, mahanila, pushparaga, brahmamani, jyotirasa, shayasaka, pearl and coral — all these are called gems. A pure diamond is born only on the bank of the Vena river. A diamond like the shirisha flower is born in the Koshala country.

**[p. 85]**

Some yellow-coloured diamond is born in the Kalinga country. The diamond born in the Paundra country is of dark colour. The deity of the six-cornered diamond is Indra. The deity of the white-coloured diamond is Yama. Of the diamonds with serpent-like faces, black, yellow and blue-coloured, the deity is Vishnu. A diamond like a woman's navel is Varuna. It is also like the karnikara flower. For the diamond like the water-chestnut or like a tiger, the deity is Agni. All diamonds of the colour of the ashoka flower or like barley have the name Vayavya. The flow of rivers etc., the mine, and the earth here and there — these three are the places of origin of the diamond. A red and yellow-coloured diamond gives auspicious fruit to the Shudra.

That diamond which, thrown by anything, does not break, which floats like rays even in ordinary water, which is glossy, which is of the colour of lightning, fire or the rainbow, is beneficial. Those diamonds in which there are crow-foot, fly, hair, metal-bearing marks, or which are without points, in all of whose corners there are two threads each, which are burnt, tarnished, lustreless and decrepit, are not auspicious-giving. Those diamonds which are cracked from the front like a bubble of water, are flat, long like a dried fruit, are also not auspicious-giving.

**Composition.** In chemical composition, diamond is pure carbon; in it there is no admixture of any other element. On burning it, the whole of it becomes carbon dioxide gas and flies away — nothing remains. On burning coloured diamonds, something remains. Acids have no effect on diamond.

**The form of naturally obtained diamond.** From mines, diamond is obtained with eight or twelve cubic faces. The Indian diamond has eight triangular faces, and the Brazilian diamond has twelve faces of parallel unequal quadrilaterals. On the triangular faces, with the help of a microscope, triangular marks and pits, just visible, are seen, and their edges and apices are in the direction opposite to the edges and apices of the face on which they are situated. On the square faces there are plates or streaks parallel to the long diagonal. These faces are somewhat raised upward (convex), and their edges are not sharp but somewhat rounded. This speciality is very helpful in its identification.

Diamonds are mostly non-conductors of electricity — on rubbing, a positive electric charge arises on the surface. But diamond lets heat pass through easily.

**[p. 86]**

For this reason it feels cold on touching — the heat of the finger moves forward (away).

Diamond is harder than all substances — no substance can scratch it, nor wear it away. In diamonds of different places, hardness is more or less. The diamonds of Borneo and Australia are harder than the African diamonds. Some such diamonds were also obtained from Africa which became hard on exposure to air. A strange thing of diamond's hardness is that one and the same diamond is harder at some places and less hard at others.

**Easily cleaving.** Though diamond is the hardest of all, yet it cleaves easily on the planes parallel to its faces. Then, on account of hardness, the belief is that the diamond cannot be broken; but if it falls by hand on the floor, it breaks. It is very brittle. It is said that on the basis of this very belief a sample obtained from South Africa was wasted. A blacksmith gave it a blow of his hammer to test it, and it scattered.

**It breaks with goat's milk.** An ancient belief is that if the diamond is kept immersed in fresh hot goat's milk, it can be easily broken. Tulsidas ji, in the Ayodhya Kanda, wrote:

> "Of all the forms of Sita-Rama's love —
> as if compassion, appearing in many forms, mourns.
> Sita's mother, saying the fate, the wit, the speech,
> with the foam of whose milk, breaking, the diamond was broken."

**The diamond burns.** If a diamond is placed in a glass vessel filled with oxygen and the sun's rays are focused on it through a burning-glass, it ignites. In the year 1816 AD, Sir Humphry Davy proved by testing with this method that diamond is pure carbon.

**Colour.** In nature the crystal of diamond is found for the most part wholly clear and transparent. On its surface there is a blazing shine, which is called "diamond lustre." A perfectly transparent and flawless one

**[p. 87]**

is called "first water," and if there is some yellowness in it, its price is not that much. Yes, if bearing a little yellowness it be colourless and transparent, its value is estimated the highest. Green-coloured diamonds are also obtained. Brown-almond-coloured diamonds come from South Africa. Diamonds of bright colours like ruby and sapphire are not obtained. In the ancient texts, from the point of view of colour, diamonds are written as of eight kinds: 1. extremely white, 2. kamlasana (lotus-seated), 3. of vegetal green colour, 4. of spring (marigold-like) colour, 5. of the blue colour of a peacock's neck, 6. dark, 7. oily (teliya), and 8. yellow-green.

**Removal of colour.** An attempt is also made to remove the colour of diamonds by giving high temperature; but on cooling, it returns again. If a diamond is kept in the blazing light of the sun and then taken into darkness, rays of the seven colours begin to come out of it. This too is one of its miraculous properties.

**The diamond is very luminous.** Place a diamond in a test-tube, remove its air by an air-exhausting machine, and pass an electric current into the test-tube. Now so much light will come out of that diamond that a book can easily be read in that light.

**Specific gravity of diamond.** According to its various kinds it is from 3.15 to 3.53. The specific gravity of a pure and clean crystal is 3.52.

**Transparency.** Only a few gems are counted in the gem category. In a cut gem, a blazing diamond lustre is found. The shine of industrial diamonds is dim. The refractive index of diamond is very high. Of the light entering substances of low refractive index, a greater quantity passes through; but in diamond, almost the whole quantity of the light that enters returns back. Therefore its sparkle is very great.

There is also the dispersion of the light ray. That is, the white ray of light, on coming out of the substance, splits into rays of seven colours — this is called the rainbow gleam or colour luminosity. In diamond, dispersion takes place in very great quantity.

**[p. 88]**

In X-rays, diamond is transparent; fake diamonds are not. In this way, with X-rays the real and fake can be identified.

**The diamond floats in water.** In ancient texts like Shukra Niti, Garuda Purana etc. it is written that the diamond which is "varitara" (floating on water) is the best. Even on being immersed in water its sparkle remains just as much as it was when placed in air. On immersion in water or bromoform or methylene iodide, an excellent diamond appears raised far above the bottom of the liquid. Synthetic sapphire and synthetic zircon etc., minerals of low refractive value, do not appear so raised on immersion in liquid, nor do they have so much shine.

According to the "Scientific Encyclopaedia," when water is mixed into the diamond-bearing silt and it is washed, the small particles of diamond appear floating on the water. The reason is that, firstly, diamond is water-repelling or hydrophobic, and then surface tension is helpful in floating the diamond. The hydrophile silt settles below.

**Auspicious diamonds.** That diamond which is colourless-clean bearing a very light blue tint, or colourless emitting blue and red rays, free from black dots, is considered auspicious and excellent. A diamond white like a conch, or shining like crystal, as interesting as the moon, and smooth, is considered of the best colour. That which, scattering red rays all around, is white, or red-yellow-white, or of the colour like the eyes of a rabbit, is a diamond of the second grade (kshatriya). That which is white with yellowness; which, on sharpening on a stone, has a shine like a sword quenched with oil or water, is a diamond of the third grade (vaishya). A white diamond with black tints is considered of the Shudra class. Anyway, all these are genuine diamonds.

**Defects of diamond.** The defects of diamond are as follows: (1) **Chhita or bindu** — having a dot or speck like water on the diamond is its blemish. If this speck is red, it is wholly to be rejected. A speck of black and white colour too makes the diamond blemished.

**[p. 89]**

(2) **Kaka-pada** — if there are black dots like the foot of a crow, that is called the crow-foot defect. Such a diamond is described as death-causing.

(3) **Yava (barley) defect** — on the diamond there can be dots of the shape of barley of four colours — white, yellow, red and black. Among these, having a white dot is auspicious. The rest are inauspicious.

(4) **Mala (stain) defect** — on the edge, the corner and the middle of the diamond — at three places the presence of stain is possible. This too is inauspicious.

(5) **Rekha (line) defect** — on the diamond there can be four kinds of lines: (a) going from the left part, (b) going from the right part, (c) crossing the line, and (d) going up after crossing the line. Among these, the first is considered excellent.

Besides these — oiliness, yellowness, brownness, a pit, a tear, absence of shine, and excessive angularity are also considered defects. On the diamond which is harder than ordinary, more difficulty arises in making facets.

**Cutting, fashioning and adornment.** As has been said above, the diamond as obtained from the mine in that form is not so attractive. Many times only a little part of the crystal is transparent, clear and of good colour. On cutting such gems properly and making facets, the value increases several times. We have described the various kinds of cuts earlier.

For cutting the diamond, nowadays the brilliant cut is used. Before this, table cut, rose cut, mine cut etc. were in use. At the end of the seventeenth century the brilliant cut came into use. This cut brings into the diamond a unique shine and sparkle. In gems of brilliant cut, besides the table facet (upper) and culet facet (lower), there are 56 facets. The table facet has an angle of about 35 to 7 with the girdle, and the culet facet of 42.

**Artificial diamond.** Gems are corrected or made by artificial method. Sometimes the non-excellent but real gems are corrected by artificial colour, treatment, heat and pressure, ray radiation by radium, special implanting,

**[p. 90]**

etc. methods! Artificial or synthetic and imitation gems are also made.

In the 69th chapter of the Garuda Purana, mentioning the diamond, it is told:

> With iron, with pushparaga, and with gomed,
> with vaidurya, sphatika and glass, in various ways
> do clever people make imitations of the vajra (diamond).

That is, clever people make fake diamonds from iron, topaz, gomed, vaidurya, crystal and glass.

These artificial diamonds can be of four kinds — 1. synthetic, 2. reconstructed, 3. imitation, and 4. doublet or triplet.

(1) **Synthetic diamond** has not been made up to now such that it could be profitable from the commercial point of view. First, in February 1955, on the particles or piece of graphite, applying a pressure of more than 1 lakh atmospheres and a temperature of 373 degrees Celsius, a synthetic diamond was made. It is said that the Russian scientists have made some machine for making fake diamonds; but up to now no such diamond has been prepared by synthetic or chemical method which could stand in comparison with the real diamond. Therefore no fear has arisen for the jeweller from the synthetic diamond as yet.

(2) **Reconstructed diamond** also could not be made up to now. To make it, the small pieces of real gems have to be melted.

(3) **Doublet diamonds** — from synthetic rutile and synthetic sapphires, doublet diamonds are made.

(4) **Imitation diamonds** — are prepared from glass and crystal.

Before describing the methods of testing for the identification of real and fake diamonds, we consider it necessary to write something on the various uses of the diamond.

**[p. 91]**

The use of the genuine diamond is for adornment, of course; at the same time, those genuine diamonds are also used for industries whose form cannot be polished or whose colour is not attractive. Those diamonds, not coming into the category of jewels, are called industrial diamonds. They are somewhat transparent and somewhat opaque. Almost all kinds of industries are now using them. In making big buildings etc., making roads and bridges, breaking stone slabs, drilling the earth to extract oil — in all such works industrial diamonds are used. The work which used to take months before is done today by machines in a moment. With diamond-set machines all work has become easy. Gem-category diamonds, even after long use, do not wear away and do not spoil; but industrial diamonds wear away and have to be replaced. These can be of four kinds — 1. micro (fine), 2. rough diamonds or bort, 3. round diamonds or ballas, and 4. black diamond or carbonado.

(1) **Fine industrial diamonds** — these are used for making tools. These diamonds are not attractive.

(2) **Rough or bort diamonds** — these are defect-ridden diamonds of irregular form. In ornamentation their use is absolutely not possible. Those small but good diamonds of which about 20 rough diamonds together make one carat in weight are used for making points for drilling. Cutting saws or rough diamonds are of the most inferior kind. Coarse powder is used for cutting glass, breaking building stone, and making other diamond tools.

(3) **Round diamond or ballas** — these are very hard and round. The round shapes made of many small crystals of diamond are called round diamond or ballas. These crystals remain attached at the radius and near the centre of the ball. They are very useful for excavation and other industrial works.

(4) **Black diamonds or carbonado** — this variety of diamond is an opaque, black or slate-coloured, very strong and dense-compact form. Their specific gravity, somewhat less than gem-diamond, is from 3.15 to 3.29. In deep excavation work and in the diamond-set tools of the lathe machine their use is made.

**[p. 92]**

The pills of diamond prepared by the gem-medicine method can be used in bloody dysentery, hemiplegia (paralysis), hoarseness of voice, cataract, creeping paralysis, painful leucorrhoea, fistula, hysteria, white discharge, diseases of the lungs and inflammation of the lungs.

**Divine power.** For the removal of the affliction of ghosts and spirits etc., and for keeping away the apprehension of poison, wearing the diamond is prescribed. A person impotent in amorous sport should wear the diamond. In the West there is the custom of giving a diamond-set ring to the newly-wed bride. It is believed that from wearing it goodwill increases; but in India, according to the statement of Shukracharya, diamond has been considered unsuitable for women. It is written:

> "A woman desiring a son should never wear the vajra (diamond)."

Under the influence of the planet Venus, that is, when the Sun is in Taurus (from 15 May to 1 June) and in Libra (from 14 October to 14 November), the gem of the persons born at that time is diamond.

The substitute of diamond is gomed. Setting a pearl in silver, it can also be worn in place of the diamond.

**Places of origin.** In London, the distribution of the rough diamond is done to the cutters and merchants. Although in South Africa many old mines have been closed, yet in their place new mines have been found and work is going on in them. The chief centres of the mines are Barkly West, Kimberley, Bosch, Jagersfontein, Barenstad. Besides these there are many more new regions where diamond stones are obtained. It is believed that nowadays more than 95 per cent of the world's production of diamond stones takes place in South Africa.

In Africa, besides South Africa, diamond stones are also obtained in South-West Africa. Here these stones are found only at a depth of one foot from the surface.

**[p. 93]**

But the gem-stones here are small and bearing some yellowness, and are not very valuable.

In Rhodesia, diamond stones are obtained in the dry valleys of old rivers. The place of origin is the Gwelo region of the Somabulo forest. Here diamond stones are obtained together with other minerals like topaz, chrysoberyl, sapphire.

In West Africa, in Gold Coast, Congo and Angola, diamond stones are also found. Here too diamonds are obtained in the valleys of dry rivers. Here the gem-stones are mostly of good water, although they are small in size. Of the stones obtained from Congo, about 66 per cent are not gem-quality.

In Australia, in New South Wales, diamond stones used to exist, but the extraction from the mines there is decreasing. In Borneo too diamond stones have continued to be obtained. The stones here are also of ten to fifteen carats. In Sierra Leone and Tanganyika too, gem-stones of excellent kind have been obtained. In Australia in Southern Queensland and in the United States of America in Arkansas, Indiana and California there are also mines of diamond stones, but their number is limited.

In India, the chief place of origin of diamond stone is the town named Panna in Madhya Pradesh. The regions of diamond obtainment in Madhya Pradesh are Panna, Chharwadi, Kothi etc. In South India, in the regions around Golconda, diamond was first found. The Golconda mine was the best in the world, but now it has been closed; however, some small diamonds are still found in the Madras province. The regions of diamond obtainment in South India are Anantapur, Bellary, Velpalli, Kothapetta, Kadapa, Gurupur, Guntur, Madgal, Mulvaram, Polilat, Gotapilli, Malpilli, Petiyal, Ustapalli, Kurnool, Bannur, Vasvapur, Gurumfot, Devanur, Dhoni, Gajetpilli, Gudiad, Madvaram and Polur.

In Orissa, in Kalahandi, Palam and near Sambalpur in Bihar some diamonds are obtained. But seeing the other production regions of the world, especially South Africa, India's production is very little.

There is much difference between the goods of Golconda and Africa. The diamond of Golconda

**[p. 94]**

is elastic and extremely attractive. In comparison, in Africa the elasticity of the diamond is less and it is not so attractive either.

**Testing and examination of diamond.** The chief minerals or gems that can create the confusion of diamond, or can be passed in its place as fake diamond, are the following: 1. synthetic rutile; 2. strontium titanate; 3. synthetic diamond; 4. imitation diamond made of glass or crystal; 5. doublet (two joined); 6. coated (layer-covered); 7. gomed; 8. synthetic white sapphire and synthetic white spinel; 9. imitation (or paste) made of lead-glass; 10. yellow or brown sphane (sphene); 11. green demantoid garnet.

The genuine diamond can be identified in the following manner:

(1) If the sample is a genuine diamond piece, then on that plane of the girdle of the "charming-shaped" diamond piece on which polishing has not been done, there will be a unique fibrous texture of the surface, or the structure of fibres. Because, while rounding the diamond piece, from the use of the lathe the surface of the diamond becomes such that no other kind of gem has such.

(2) On the girdle of a cut-and-dressed diamond piece, or on the natural skin near it, "natural" parts certainly remain. On these natural parts pits or triangular shapes appear, which are found respectively on parallel unequal quadrilateral faces and on triangular faces. On the faces of any kind of fake diamond piece these marks will not be there.

(3) A well-cut diamond has its own special sparkle, which is called "diamond lustre." Such sparkle is not in any other gem. The sparkle of gomed is not diamond-lustre but like chrysolite's. On careful seeing it is known.

(4) Diamond is the hardest mineral. If the gem-piece is gently scratched — if a light scratch falls on the artificial sapphire or ruby piece, then the tested gem-piece is a genuine diamond. But diamond is brittle.

**[p. 95]**

(5) In a genuine diamond piece, the light that enters returns back almost wholly from within; it does not pass through. Place a finger behind the gem-piece you are testing and try to see your finger from the front. If the tested gem-piece is genuine, the finger will not appear — the light falling on the finger in the genuine diamond piece, not passing through the piece, will return back, and you will not see the finger.

(6) From the diamond, the refraction (scattering into the seven rainbow colours) of the light ray takes place in excessive quantity. If it is a genuine diamond, then on looking within through the top facet, the rainbow gleam of colours will not appear. Among colourless gems, it is found only in gomed. But in white gomed there is double refraction; therefore its back facets appear doubled; and from the test of hardness the confusion of gomed is removed.

Now we indicate the methods of testing of some special substitutes of diamond:

1. **Strontium titanate** looks like diamond, and its optical properties also match those of diamond. Colourless, cubic, single refraction (refractive index 2.4), refraction about four times that of diamond, the play of rainbow colours also more than diamond. Its density is 5.3. But its hardness is 5. Well, from this deficiency of hardness alone its identification apart from diamond is made. Pierce the given sample with a thin point of iron wire and see. If it is strontium titanate, a light mark will fall on it — no harm will come to the sample. Then on throwing ultraviolet or X-rays on strontium, fluorescence will not appear. In X-rays it remains opaque. In a genuine diamond both will be the opposite.

2. **Synthetic diamonds** — up to now only industrial ones could be made, not gems. Therefore for the jeweller there is no problem of these.

**[p. 96]**

3. **Imitation diamonds of glass** — in shine, the imitation diamonds made of glass also seem real diamonds, but their refractive index is lower. Therefore, by knowing the refractive index through the refractometer, the test is made. The hardness of real gems is more. Therefore the sample used to be cut with a file and seen; but if the sample is a genuine diamond, even it can be damaged by the file.

Besides this, an imitated sample of glass will feel hotter on touching in comparison with the real gem — because glass is a less good conductor of heat than diamond. Testing by touching with the tongue will be more reliable, because the skin of the tongue is thinner. Take care — clean the sample and hold it with tongs, lest the heat of the hand reach it. This is an extremely useful test.

In the imitation of glass there can be one or more bubbles. In lead-glass they are mostly oval and separate — not bunched together.

In the imitation of glass there are usually swirls, because the mixing of the batch remains incomplete. When syrup is mixed with water, just such swirls appear.

In the imitation of glass, refraction never appears.

4. **Doublet (two joined)** — these have three classes. (a) **True doublet** — in the doublets of this class, both the crown and the base are of one and the same stone. In them there is only the plan of joining two pieces. These are found less. (b) In the doublet of the second class, the crown and base are of one and the same stone, but between them a surface of glass is tightly filled, in which the desired colour comes. These are sometimes also called triplets. (c) In the doublet of the third class, the front facet is of the real stone. The bases are of glass of various colours. This class is the most current.

**Test.** Only on careful viewing with a lens does the joining line appear. If the pieces are not joined by melting — if they are joined with cement, then on putting in alcohol they will separate. With the refractometer the joint is easily caught. If the crown of the diamond is joined to a base of rock-crystal, then, though shining, flame will not appear in it. Tilt the stone and see; on the joining plane a shadow will appear.

**Testing with machines.** For the correct evaluation of the properties of diamond, for measuring hardness, specific gravity, refraction, dispersion etc., the refractometer etc. instruments and magnifying lenses etc. apparatus are also used.

**[p. 97]**

**Historical diamonds.** Well, from time to time such diamonds have been found whose names have become immortal on account of their proofs or other qualities. Some diamonds have proved to be weep-inducing on account of their weakness. Many have been the cause of bloodshed. Among the history-famous diamonds obtained from India, some are the following — Koh-i-noor, Regent, Orlov, Sancy, Shah, Nassak, Pigot, Eugenie, Hope, the Transvaal Green and the Florentine Yellow.

(1) **Koh-i-noor** — the famous diamond of India — in the year 130 AD it was known. Until the invasion of Nadir Shah it remained the property of the Mughal emperor. After Nadir Shah it is the adornment of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, and after him of the British crown. Original weight 186.5 carats, now 106.1 carats.

(2) **Pitt or Regent diamond** — obtained in the year 1701 AD at about 150 miles from Golconda. William Pitt bought it at that time for 20,400 pounds. Original weight 410 carats, after cutting 136.9 carats. Now displayed in the Louvre, in the Apollo Gallery in Paris.

(3) **Orlov** — made by cutting the Great Mogul obtained from the Kollur mines in the year 1650 AD, stolen from the eyes of the idol of Brahmaji situated in a temple in Mysore; now fixed at the upper end of the Russian imperial sceptre. Weight 195 carats.

(4) **Sancy** — in the year 1477 AD a robber soldier snatched it from the corpse of Charles the Bold; obtained from Sancy, at the end of the 16th century it was sold to Queen Elizabeth; in 1695 James II sold it to Louis XIV; in 1791 the weight was 53.75 carats; with the gems of the French crown it was stolen away — then it was not found.

**[p. 98]**

(5) **Shah** — an elongated octahedral-shaped, well-cut diamond of 88.7 carats weight. In 1843 it became the property of the Russian Tsar Nicholas.

(6) **Nassak** — in the beginning a pear-shaped diamond of 89.75 carats weight, now a triangular diamond of 78.6 carats weight, obtained by the English in a loot in South India. Now perhaps in the possession of the descendants of the Duke of Westminster.

(7) **Pigot** — of 47.5 carats weight; in 1775, Lord Pigot took it to England. Later in the possession of the ruler of Egypt, Ali Pasha — after his death it was destroyed.

(8) **Eugenie** — the eye-attracting diamond — passing from the Empress of Russia, through Napoleon, later became the property of the Maharaja of Baroda.

(9) **Hope** — the largest among coloured diamonds, weight 44.5 carats; colour bluish-green; obtained from the Kollur mines; in 1642 the famous French jeweller Tavernier obtained it in India; in 1792 it was stolen along with the gems of the French crown. In 1830 the London merchant Hope bought it, somewhat cut. Then in America, France, and now in England. This diamond has proved inauspicious for its owner.

(10) **Easton** — a clear, well-cut diamond of the colour of a green apple. Weight 40 carats; in 1743 Augustus bought it.

Besides these, the Kuliyanan, the Great Table, the Zaker, the Star of South Africa, etc. — how many diamonds have become famous in the world.

**Various names.** Sanskrit: nila, shauri-ratna, indranila, trunagrhi, nilamani etc. Hindi: neelam. Urdu-Persian: neelam, yaqut, kabud. English: sapphire.

**Physical properties.** Hardness 9; specific gravity 4.03; refractive index 1.76–1.77; double refraction 0.008; dichroism very distinct; brittle; dispersion less than that of diamond. For this reason its sparkle and brilliance are less than diamond's, and on this basis the difference between white sapphire and diamond is told. Sapphire is a compound of aluminium and oxygen. From cobalt remaining mixed in small quantity, the colour becomes blue.

**The corundum group.** As we have written in the chapter on "Ruby," both ruby and sapphire are gems of the corundum group. In reality, red corundum becomes "ruby," and the gems of the other colours

**[p. 100]**

are called sapphire — such as white, green, violet, yellow etc. Our own opinion is this — as is clear from the name, only that gem should be called sapphire which is of blue, sky-blue, violet etc. colour — whether the colour be light or deep, no difference should be counted in the name from that. Whether we call white sapphire "sapphire" in books, it will in reality be obtained in the jewellers' market under the name of white topaz. Similarly, what we call yellow sapphire is sold in the market under the name of yellow topaz alone; and the interesting thing is that the real yellow topaz, which is called topaz, is not considered so good as the yellow topaz of the corundum group (you may, if you wish, happily call it yellow sapphire). We have never heard anyone in the jewellers' market call a green corundum gem a green sapphire. We believe that the green-coloured corundum gem is sold under the name of emerald. In the books of western authors, green sapphire has been given the name of "Oriental emerald." If all were sapphire, then why is the red gem called ruby? It too should be called sapphire.

**Star sapphire.** Like star ruby, there are also star sapphires, which are called "star sapphire." When these gems are cut in cabochon cut, at their summit appears the light of a star of six rays. This star-light appears as if a fountain of light-rays were gushing forth in a blue sky. Star rubies are fewer; star sapphires appear in greater number. Sapphires are found in larger size than rubies. In the year 1930 a sapphire of 1000 carats was obtained; but since ruby is more rare, if rubies are more numerous and valuable, the value of sapphire is much less than ruby's. There is one more difference between these two. Although both gems are brittle and there is the need of caution with them at all times, ruby is less hard than sapphire, and the hardness is different in the sapphire gems obtained from different places of origin. The sapphires obtained from Sri Lanka are harder than those obtained from Kashmir. The specific gravity of all the gems of the corundum group is one and the same, and the dichroism is so distinct that it can be seen without a magnifying lens. The dispersion of colour is less. Therefore the sparkle in it is also less.

**[p. 101]**

By this single property alone, white topaz and diamond can be told apart.

Sapphires appear in brilliant, mixed, step and cabochon cut (mostly only star sapphires are cut in cabochon cut). The cutting of the Sri Lanka sapphire is such that all the light that comes out passes through the blue spot in the plane of the gem.

**Places of origin.** It is believed that the most beautiful rubies come from Burma, and from Sri Lanka the sapphires are the best and in abundant quantity. But the real fact is that neither are all rubies obtained from Burma excellent, nor all sapphires from Sri Lanka. It is acknowledged that the sapphires obtained from the Kashmir province of India are the best. In Thailand, deep-coloured sapphires are obtained. Deep blue and green-blue sapphires have also been found in Australia. In Sri Lanka, sapphires are found in various kinds of rocks. Here they are found with garnets in the gneiss rock. In Sri Lanka the gem regions are Balangoda, Rakwana and Ratnapura. These regions are situated in the southern-middle part of Sri Lanka. In Ratnapura there is a big market of gems. In deep narrow valleys, pits are dug in the alluvial deposit beside the water-channels. After reaching various depths through mud, clay and sand, the gem-bearing gravel, which is called "illam," is obtained. Its thickness is from 5 to 6 feet, and it lies upon the rocks. After digging out the illam, placing it in shallow baskets, it is washed in the nearby stream. Then, separating the clean gravel, the gems are picked out from it. In this area, besides sapphire, some other corundum of various colours and several kinds of precious stones and gold are obtained. The gems and gold are taken out of the flowing water-channels by a net fixed in an iron frame. From the gravels situated at a height beside the valleys too, gems are sometimes obtained.

In the Kashmir province, the mine of sapphire is near the village named Sumjam at a height of 14,540 feet. Its associate mineral is tourmaline. Regarding the sapphire gems of Kashmir, first of all there is an entertaining story. It is said that the knowledge of the sapphire mine was obtained by chance by some travellers who were coming from Afghanistan to Delhi. On the way, from a mountain,

**[p. 102]**

some rocks had fallen in the rain. In those scattered rocks many attractive blue-coloured stones appeared lying. They took salt, as needed, in exchange for all those stones. Through how many hands these stones passed around, and much later they were recognized as sapphire. Then they were sold at quite expensive prices. When the then king of Kashmir received this news, he seized as many gems as could be obtained. Later, in beautiful form, they reached the Maharaja's treasury.

Sapphires are also found in Queensland and New South Wales (Australia) and Rhodesia. In India, besides Kashmir, in South India in the Salem district sapphires are obtained. In Russia and in the United States of America too there are mines of sapphire.

The Kashmir sapphire is the best, this we have already told. It is called the "Mayur (peacock) sapphire," because its colour is of the colour of a peacock's neck. In it, if there is even one dot of colour, it keeps the whole gem coloured. But only if it is found free from that sting, hollow and two-colouredness, does its gem become beautiful. Then in it a foreign substance is stuck. The Kashmir sapphire does not change its colour in electric light, whereas the sapphires obtained from other places appear of navy-blue colour in electric light.

In the Burma sapphire there is less greenness and a beautiful blue colour. Since no foreign substance is stuck in it, there is facility in making the gem.

The sapphire of Sri Lanka is of an inferior grade than both the sapphires written above. In it there is a red lustre; there is also much dark lustre.

In the sapphire of Siam there is much blackness of colour and greenness. On account of the deep colour it appears black. In it there is the most hardness and smoothness.

In the sapphires of Salem (South India) there is more greenness than in Siam's. A yellow and blue colour is mixed.

The sapphires of Australia are of deep blue colour.

**[p. 103]**

The sapphires of Rhodesia and Russia are of inferior colour.

Topaz, sapphire and ruby are almost all found in one and the same region. This is the very reason that there is an intermixture of the colours of these stones among themselves. For this reason, although naturally the white and yellow colour of topaz, the red of ruby and the blue colour of sapphire are there, yet from the influence of one another's colour coming in, there appears in topaz a blue or pink lustre, in sapphire a red lustre (this is called "raktamukhi" sapphire), and in ruby a blue lustre. Sometimes, indeed, in one and the same stone all three colours appear separately; but remember that despite so much similarity, in the hardness of these three stones there is difference. In comparison with topaz, ruby, and in comparison with ruby, sapphire, are more hard.

The deep-blue-coloured sapphires, being rare, are extremely valuable. There are also orange-coloured sapphires which appear shining like the sun. Their full beauty comes only when they are set with diamonds. Green, golden and violet-coloured sapphires are by mistake taken to be emerald, topaz and amethyst, although they are wholly different from them. In reality green sapphire is neither like emerald, nor does it resemble green peridot or tourmaline. It is of deep colour, and on account of good hardness a polish dazzling to the eyes can be made on it. It has its own special splendid personality, and when it is in attractive cut form and set with diamonds, its beauty is worth seeing. When the violet sapphire is clear and in a fine cut form, amethyst appears like a lamb before it. There is no doubt that golden sapphire resembles topaz, but on account of more hardness in it the polish is very beautiful, and before such a polished gem the yellow topaz appears washed-out.

Sapphires are from transparent to translucent and opaque.

**[p. 104]**

**Testing and examination.**

**Defects of sapphire:** green-lustred, dora (thread), dudhak (milky), chir (tear), duranga (two-coloured), jala (web), gaddha (pit), pol (hollow), gum, bhailapan (shyness), dank (sting).

**The best sapphire** is that which has seven specialities: (1) it should be one which, not taking the shadow of another substance, shines it with its own shine, or appears of one and the same colour; (2) be sparkling; (3) smooth; (4) of transparent shine; (5) whose body is compact; (6) on touching feels soft; and (7) from within which rays seem to burst forth.

On the night of the full moon, when the clear moonlight of the blooming moon has spread, a beautiful fair-complexioned woman should be given a bowl filled with clean milk in her hand, and the light of the sapphire should be cast on that vessel. If the sapphire, with its light, covers the curd, the milk vessel and the beauty at once with a blue lustre, then the sapphire should be understood to be of the excellent kind. Another speciality of an excellent sapphire is that on bringing a straw near it, it sticks to it.

**Imitation sapphire.** Like ruby, synthetic sapphire, blue-coloured zircon gem and glass imitations are sold. The methods given in the chapter on ruby for the identification of real and fake ruby should be applied here too. In artificial sapphire there are curved (bent) plates of colours. In real sapphire these streaks are straight.

**Identifying real and fake.** In place of sapphire, people make the effort to pass off synthetic sapphire, imitation sapphire made of glass, and synthetic zircon gem of blue colour.

By seeing the inner structure of the sample being tested, the secret of the sapphire's being synthetic opens up. Immersing it in monobromo-naphthalene liquid and placing white paper or cloth behind it, see with the naked eye alone — (1) In the synthetic, curved plates of colours will appear. In the real one these will be straight, or parallel to the faces of the hexagonal prism. (2) In a real naturally

**[p. 105]**

obtained sapphire, some mineral substances remain stored within, which, according to the difference of place, are various objects. In the Lanka sapphire there is a feather-like subtle crystal or liquid substance, and the light reflected falling on it appears such as if it were the mark of a thumb. Sometimes the substance stored within is, in this way, a gas bubble, liquid and crystals of minerals — three-formed.

**Historical sapphires.** Possibly the big known sapphires have come from the mines of Kashmir alone. The sapphire which was in the treasury of the Rewa state in the year 1827 was the largest, and in weight was 951 carats. In the collection of John D. Platin there are two excellent sapphires — of these, one named "Ruspoli," a very beautiful and flawless sapphire, is of 122 carats. The second sapphire is 2 inches long and 1.50 inches wide. With the Duke of Devonshire there is a beautiful brilliant-cut sapphire of 100 carats weight. In the mineral department of the British Museum, the idol of Lord Buddha placed on a golden pin has been made by cutting a single sapphire. The largest, of about 132 carats weight, a brown-coloured sapphire, is in the mineral museum of Paris. It was found by some wood-seller in Bengal. It was once in the possession of Darnley, the husband of Mary, Queen of Scots. A heart-shaped sapphire is now in the British royal crown. It is said to be of the year 157 AD. Two sapphires, in the form of amulets, were presented to Napoleon by a priest. These later passed into the possession of Louis Napoleon III.

**Jalnila and Indranila (ancient belief).** According to the ancient Indian texts, sapphire is of two kinds — jalnila and indranila. That sapphire within which there is whiteness, and all around blueness, which is light, is called jalnila. That sapphire within which there is a dark lustre, outside blueness, which is heavy, is called indranila. In reality its colour is blue and red mixed, that is, violet.

**Various names.** Sanskrit: gomed, gomedaka, pinga-sphatika, rahu-ratna. Hindi: gomed. Urdu-Persian: jarkuniya or jargun. Arabic: jarkun (vermilion-coloured). English: zircon.

**Physical properties.** Hardness up to 7.5. Specific gravity from 4.65 to 4.71. Transparent, translucent and also opaque. Diamond lustre. Refractive index from 1.93 to 1.98; double refraction 0.06; dispersion 0.048 (quite high).

**Zircon** — such an attractive and entertaining gem-stone, which is obtained in almost all captivating colours. Our opinion is that it would not be proper to call every colour of zircon "gomed." Only that zircon should be called gomed which is of the colour of honey, cow's urine or an ember. In support of our opinion, with thanks to Shri Rajrup Tick's "Ratna Prakash," the following verses are given:

**[p. 107]**

> Susvachchham gojalchhayam svachchham snigdhvam samam guruh:
> Nirmalam masrinam diptam gomedam shubhamashtadha.
> Vichchhayam laghu rukshangam chipitam patalanvitam,
> Nishprabham pitakachabham gomedam na shubhavaham.

That is — that which, from a distance, appears of the colour of clean cow's urine, is transparent, of glossy colour, of even body — that is, not high-low and crooked-crooked; which is heavy, sparkling, in which there is no layer, which is soft in touch and shining. A gomed having eight qualities in this way is excellent. That which from a distance gives no light, is not sparkling, bears dryness, appears flat-bodied and pressed, is layered, devoid of shine — that is, which has no shine illuminating the colour — and appears like a piece of yellow glass, that gomed is not excellent.

Nowadays, just as the practice of calling every colour of sapphire "neelam" has become current, so too every colour of zircon is called gomed. The gomed which is considered the rahu-gem is that very gem whose description, and its excellence and defects, we have given above in the verse.

As said before, zircon is found in many beautiful colours, and slowly it is obtaining a respectable place in ornaments. Chemically it is the silicate of zirconium, and its crystals are like four-sided prisms whose ends are in the form of four-sided pyramids. When these are in the form of water-worn pebbles, their faces get worn away. These gem-stones come under the tetragonal system. They are generally doubly-terminated and have four-fold symmetry. Zircon is also found in round or angular lumps and grains. Its cleavage lines, which are columnar or pyramidal, are imperfect. In it there is a conchoidal fracture. The hardness of zircon is different in each gem, but it is not more than 7.

**[p. 108]**

Zircons are of all kinds — transparent, translucent and opaque. In it diamond lustre is found. On account of strong double refraction, on viewing zircon from various directions, the back facets clearly appear doubled. Zircons generally display uniaxial and positive optical properties, and are double-axis. Of the many colours of zircon, the chief colours are green, yellow, orange, brown. The gem-stones which are red or red-brown are called hyacinth or jacinth, those in which there is yellowness are called jargon. Colourless zircons are fewer, and those which appear in the market are in reality yellow or brown gem-stones whose colour has been removed by giving heat. In these white zircons there is a tremendous sparkle, and if any gem-stone reaches near the diamond in shine and sparkle, it is zircon alone. Although several kinds of zircon are dull and opaque, even then sparkle certainly appears in them. On account of its various colours, zircon resembles several kinds of gem-stones, but by their specific density they can be separated from them. It is quite high — from 4.65 to 4.71. Just as the specific gravity of the various stones of zircon is different, so too the refractive index of the various stones of zircon is different. In some there is single refraction, in some double refraction. Dichroism is found only in the blue-coloured gem-stone.

The properties of the real stones of zircon differ from one another so much that the necessity appeared to divide them into three classes: (1) high class, (2) middle class, (3) low class. The hardness, specific gravity, optical specialities and heat-behaviour etc. properties of the zircons of all three classes are different.

(1) **High class** — this zircon is generally the famous gomed. Its crystal is four-sided. The physical properties given above are of the gems of this class. Its dichroism is so much that it can be seen with the eye. In it two colours, blue and white, appear. It does not cleave easily. Its dispersion is also a little less than diamond's, 0.038. Therefore in it there is an extraordinary sparkle.

**[p. 109]**

(2) **Middle class** — this is between the low and high classes. It is of deep red colour and red colour bearing brownness. On heating, with some change in double refraction, refractive index and sparkle, it becomes of the high class.

(3) **Low class** — its form is not the form of a crystal. It is found in green tints. It is also found in brown and orange colours. Its refractive index is low. Dichroism is also very little.

**The unique property of zircon.** The unique property of this gem is that it sometimes shows absorption plates in the spectroscope instrument.

**Cutting.** Zircons are cut in various kinds of cut, but the brilliant cut is adopted more.

**Places of origin.** The best zircons are obtained in Sri Lanka, where they are found together with other gem-stones. These are of beautiful blue or green colour. In Sri Lanka, in the beginning they were understood to be diamond, and there even now they are given the name of "Matura diamond," because they are obtained from the place named Matura. These gem-stones, especially the red-coloured zircon, are obtained from the place Madgi in New South Wales, Australia, and from France and Russia. In India too zircon is found, which is blue and blue-green. The places of origin of India's zircon are Kashmir, Kullu, Shimla, near Hazaribagh in the Bihar province, Travancore, Coimbatore. From Thailand, blue and white zircons used to come at one time. They are obtained from the sapphire mines there. It is said that after the year 1921 these gem-stones do not come out. Those which are exported from there are in reality obtained from the mines of Cambodia. In Madagascar too zircons are obtained. Yellow and brown zircons are found with diamond in the Kimberley mines of South Africa. According to Ratna Prakash, in Burma too, at the place named Mogok, zircon is found. In it there is more water and elasticity and a small quantity of dark lustre. The goods here are considered the best, but they are obtained very little.

**[p. 110]**

**Testing and examination.**

White gomed is colourless and in sparkle equals the diamond. Therefore even an inexperienced jeweller has the confusion of its being diamond. In the form of a diamond ring this deception is very much in vogue.

Its clear identification is this, that in it there is double refraction, and on this account the edges of the back facets appear doubled on viewing from the front. In diamond this does not happen.

The second gem-stone equal to gomed is sphene — but its hardness is very low, 5.5; on comparing the hardness, both can be known apart.

Sometimes, on the back plane of an unattractive-coloured inferior diamond piece, this artificial blue colour — on the dye falling into benzene, spirit or merely hot water — dissolves. Well, its specific gravity is anyway much higher than diamond's. By the comparison of specific gravity its identification can be made.

**The largest zircon.** The heaviest gem that has been found up to now weighed 25 pounds.

**Defects of zircon:** rough blister; mica-like; pit, tear, stain; two-coloured; with red-black-white specks; gum; fibre; web.

**Various names.** Sanskrit: vaidurya, vidalaksha, abhrahroha, rashtuk, meghakhuragur, balasurya, vidura-ratna. Hindi: lahsuniya. Urdu-Persian: lahsuniya. English: cat's-eye or chrysoberyl.

**Physical properties.** Hardness 8.50; specific gravity from 3.68 to 3.78. Refractive index from 1.750 to 1.757; double refraction 0.010; dispersion 0.015. Its structure is positive.

Lahsuniya, or vaidurya, which is called chrysoberyl in English, is a variety of it. We hold that vaidurya is a yellow-coloured gem. In darkness the various colours of the cat's-eye glimmer. It is made by cutting a fibrous stone. Hemavaidurya is the aluminate of beryllium. The colour of hemavaidurya is light yellow, brown and green. Its flawless gem-stones are in the Mogok mines of Upper Burma and in the state of Ghana in West Africa. Its green colour bearing a light yellowness is due to ferrous oxide. The jewellers have given the name of chrysolite to gems of this kind. The colour of the lahsuniya (vidalaksha) is mostly bearing brownness. Vidalaksha is always given the form of the cabochon cut. Hemavaidurya is the hardest gem after diamond and corundum. It has a glassy lustre. In the cat's-eye there is a silk-like shine. Hemavaidurya is transparent and translucent.

**[p. 112]**

The display of various colours in the cat's-eye in darkness is very curious. The shine that is in the gem is like the eyes of a cat. In it there is a silk-like lustre and a green colour. When cut in cabochon cut, the light appears concentrated in a line or band, and it plays on the surface of the gem. On turning, the line of light, which seems like a thread, changes. In this very way, from the moving and playing of the line, the eyes of a cat come to mind. On account of this moving thread-line, the gem also got the name of "sutra-mani" (thread-gem). This effect takes place on account of the fibrous structure of the gem. The cat's-eye gem has now become very popular. The reason for this is its attractive colour, curiosity, and durability.

Cat's-eye gems are found mostly in pegmatite, gneiss and micaceous layered rocks. They are also found in the valleys of streams. Its chief places of origin are Sri Lanka, Brazil and China. The cat's-eye gem of the Mogok mine of Burma is considered excellent. In Trivandrum (South India) too this gem is found. In the British Museum, with a chrysolite, a beautiful cat's-eye 35.5 millimetres long and 12 millimetres wide is kept.

**Testing and examination.**

That cat's-eye (lahsuniya) whose colour is yellow-lustred and which has a white thread (like a dora), is considered excellent. The more shining this thread is and the straighter like a line, the more excellent is the gem. If the thread, not being like a line, is spread out, then it is called a "chadder" (sheet). That lahsuniya in which there is no thread is called "katketak" (cymophane).

**[p. 113]**

According to the ancient beliefs, that lahsuniya which bears a black and white mixed lustre, is of even body, is clean, sparkling, glistening, and is wombed in a white dupaṭṭa (shawl) like the autumnal cloud — that is, in which, around the neck, a white-coloured thread is seen, straight like a dupaṭṭa lying with its two edges clearly visible, but with roundness — is considered auspicious and excellent.

**Defects:** stain, pit, tear, mica-like web, speck, dullness.

**Classes of the species:** (1) **Kanak-khet** — the best species. Its colour is exactly like the eye of a cat. (2) **Dhumra-khet** — with a white thread of the colour of dhumra (smoke). (3) **Krishna-khet** — with a white thread bearing a dark lustre.

**Colours:** yellow-lustred white, dark-lustred; in it a blue and green lustre is also found in small quantity.

**River cat's-eye.** Its colour is yellow-mixed dark, and in this very colour there is a thread of deep colour. Its form is like the eye of a tiger.

**The cat's-eye of the crystal class.** This too is, in optical effect, like the vidalaksha, the variety of hemavaidurya, but it is a very soft stone. It is mostly cut in cabochon. Many of its gem-stones have an artificial colouring, but those colours are not permanent. Its natural colour has a green lustre or a green-brown lustre. Besides cheap ornaments, these are used in making umbrella handles. Its chief places of origin are India and Sri Lanka.

### Opal (Upal)

Opal is called "opal" in English. The white opal is also called the milky stone. Its physical properties are: hardness 6; specific gravity 2.15; refractive index 1.45.

Opal is an extremely beautiful gemstone, and in every way different from other gemstones. It is considered difficult to make artificial or synthetic opal. Its ever-changing colours bring it into the class of valuable gemstones, but remember that not all opals are valuable. Beautiful and eye-delighting black opals, which are extremely rare gems, can rival any valuable gem in beauty and in price. On the contrary, the white opal, which is not at all rare, is counted among the semi-precious gems. But white opals that have the changeability of colours and sparkle do become valuable.

**[p. 115]**

**Opal's unique structure.** In the chemical composition of opal the element silica is predominant. It also contains 6 to 10 percent water. Because of the presence of metallic oxides in opal there is also a plenty of impurities, but the miracle of marvellous colours that is in it is due to its structure. Opal is not crystalline in form, but in its natural state is a jelly-like mass of silica which, after cooling and hardening, develops cracks. These fine cracks later fill with the opal substance itself, in which there is also water in various quantities. In this way solid masses carrying several refractive indices are prepared, and their play of colours and their glitter is called "opalescence" in English. The finer and more regularly formed the crack, the more beautiful is its coloured optical effect. In some opal gems the cracks contain only air, no solid substance. In such gems the play of colours is not seen until they are immersed in water. Opal of this kind is called hydrophane.

**Classes of opal.** Valuable opals are of two kinds — black and white. Both kinds of stones display colours beautifully, but those in which there is an abundance of red glow (alaka) are more prized and more valuable. Some black opals are completely opaque and in them there are many grey and blue spots. The pure gemstones are mostly colourless. The stones of the yellow-milky kind are considered quite ordinary, and being unattractive their demand is very little. Commercially, in the first class are placed those opals from which an orange glow comes forth. The green opals in which a wave of various colours is formed come second in value, and the blue are the cheapest. Grey and lustreless white are considered useless.

There is also a transparent kind of opal in which there is no sparkle. It has been given the name of fire opal. It is of colour from yellow to yellow-red, and is cut in brilliant cut.

**[p. 116]**

When placed among other gems in ornaments, it looks good.

The water opals of Mexico are like drops of burning coloured water. They are extremely beautiful, and in a single gem there is a very delightful display of many colours. But in prices they cannot equal the valuable black and white gems.

Meb Wilson, a recognised gem-expert of England, wrote in his book, mentioning opal, that opals are of about sixteen kinds, but of them only one comes in the class of valuable gems. The rest are some that can be called attractive. Among the gem-class opals the most valuable are the black and white opals, but in reality they are neither entirely black nor entirely white. In the white opal the marvel of red, blue, green, violet and yellow colours is seen. The black opal has the same attraction, but less than the white. The black opal was first obtained in 1905, and it is found only in the mines of Lightning Ridge on the border of New South Wales and Queensland in Australia. This opal is usually black, grey, peacock-coloured and blue, and in it there are emerald-like green parts and streaks of red, yellow and violet colour.

The blue or peacock opal is either entirely blue or has blue, yellow and green mixed in it. In the green opal there are green waves as it were whirling. There is one opal which is called "harlequin". It cannot be called of any one colour, because in it on a red-black ground the spring of other colours appears. Turning it in the hand, now one colour shines, now another. This gem performs a sort of magic show. There is an opal, the hydrophane, which is a dull-looking, woe-begone-faced sort of gem. If it is dropped into water, it is compelled to display its colours. Hyalite opal is colourless and transparent, but looks like glass. It is the most unattractive member of the opal family.

**[p. 117]**

The fire opal of the opal family is worthy of being given the brilliant cut. It is found in Mexico. It is red, orange-like or yellow. In some stones all three colours are together.

Among the semi-precious opals there are some attractive varieties. The fern opal is coloured like the delicate green of a leaf. Quinzite ranges from pink to red. Girasol opal has a blue-white shimmer. Milky opals are found in many whites, such as white with a blue tint, white with a yellow tint, white with a green tint, etc. They are also entirely of milky colour.

Pliny said that opal is such a beautiful gem that the living glow of ruby, the violet tint of amethyst and the sea-like green of emerald all combined appear shining in it in a magical form.

But beware! Opal is not a hard gemstone. It can easily be damaged, and can also be scratched. Besides, because of its porosity, it should not be immersed in any liquid. Heat has a bad effect on the colour of opal. To bring out the complete beauty of opal it is given the cabochon cut, but as we said above, the fire opal is given the brilliant cut.

**Places of origin.** The oldest mines of opal are in Hungary, and the Romans were attracted to Hungary. But the production of the Hungarian mines is now almost finished. Its production centres are New South Wales and Queensland in Australia. In Australia this gem was found by chance. It is said that in 1889, when a hunter was running after a kangaroo, he caught sight of this delightful gemstone. Later many mines of this gem were discovered. The chief mines are in the Queensland and New South Wales fields, and from these most of the opals are obtained. Even now, if anything does come from the Hungarian mines, only small gemstones are obtained. In Queensland and New South Wales the gemstones are found in sandstone, in the cracks of rocks or of wood. There are also obtained gemstones in which there are layers of opal of several colours in one single stone. These are called "opal agate". Some jasper-coloured opals are also found, which are called jasper opal. The biggest buyer of Australian opal gemstones is China.

Gem-class opals are also obtained in the country of Honduras and in the state of New Jersey of the United States of America. Fire opal is found only in Mexico. In some states of Mexico, especially Queretaro and Zimapan, opals of various colours are found. Its other places there were Hidalgo, Zacatecas, Irapuato and Chihuahua. Some beautiful black opals are in Humboldt County of the state of Nevada in the U.S.A.

**Some famous opals.** The most beautiful and highest-grade opal of the modern age was with Empress Josephine. Because of the extraordinary display of the many red glows coming out of it, its name was kept "Burning of Troy". In the collection of Vienna is the largest but flawed opal, which weighs seventeen ounces and is about the size of a man's fist. In 1919 a large black opal was found in the state of Nevada of the U.S.A., whose weight was 18 ounces and whose value was twenty-five thousand pounds. It was not cut, and in its natural form was kept in the National Museum of the U.S.A.

**Libyan desert glass.** This is also a form of silica, and resembles opal in this way that it is also non-crystalline and isotropic in its optical properties. In it there is no water at all, as in opal. Desert glass is mostly obtained from the desert of Libya. It is of a pale green-yellow colour. It is cloudy or clear. The largest piece of desert glass weighed 16 pounds. When cut it looks like a light-coloured olivine. Because its refractive index is low (which is 1.4624), it does not have the olivine-like sparkle.

**[p. 119]**

Deep brown and black desert glasses have been found in Australia, the East Indies, Malaysia and Indonesia. These are called tektites. People's opinion is that their place of origin is meteorites fallen from the sky.

### Spinel (Ketakija)

Semi-precious, but on account of colour fetching a fair price: a captivating colour: varieties: the difference between spinel and ruby: places of origin: historical and famous spinels: the ornament of the royal crown: artificial spinel.

**Various names.** Spinel is called ketakija in Hindi. It is also called spinel ruby, lalari and suryamani. Some jewellers call it only by the name naram (soft).

**Physical properties.** Hardness 8; specific gravity 3.40; refractive index 1.72. It is the double oxide of magnesium and aluminium. This mineral is found in the form of crystals which come under the cubic system. It is found in octahedral solid forms or twin octahedral solid forms.

**The different kinds of spinel.** The spinel crystals used in ornaments are called semi-precious spinel. They are found in many colours. It is said that there is perhaps no colour which is not found in this gem. The pink spinels are called balas ruby. There is great demand for them, and so they are of greater value. The other colours of spinel are also quite attractive, and to them the following names have been given: (1) Ruby spinel (lalari) — these are gemstones red like ruby. (2) Almandine spinel — these are yellow. (3) Pleonaste and ceylonite — these range from deep green to black. (4) Sapphire spinel — these are of sapphire colour. (5) Rubicelle — these range from red to orange.

**[p. 120]**

Spinels are mostly transparent, but in them the property of cleavage (splitting easily) is almost nil. Their hardness is like that of topaz, so they are durable and can be used in rings etc. This gem does not come in the class of valuable gems, though by its hardness it deserves that position, because being a gem of the cubic system its refraction is single only, and for this very reason no dichroism is seen in it. By these properties it can be separated from tourmaline and ruby, though in appearance it often looks just like them. In reality many gems that have been taken to be rubies are actually spinels. In this connection an interesting thing is that the rubies, which have the honour of being included among the great gems, are mostly flawed; on the contrary, spinels are mostly clear and flawless. Though spinel gems have a glassy lustre, in them there is little brilliance. These are in the brilliant, step and mixed cuts. But while setting these gems complete care is needed, because they are brittle.

In nature spinel or ketakija or suryamani has always been a companion of corundum. It is found in those very rocks in which corundum is found, and it is formed under similar conditions, but for it a little magnesia is required to combine with alumina.

According to the English gem-expert Meb Wilson, a spinel of more than six-seven carats is a rare gem. In hardness it is below ruby and sapphire and above emerald. For this reason, with good polish it acquires a brilliant glow. Though spinels are obtained in many colours, only the red spinel has been able to attain special esteem, because it looks like ruby. Blue and violet spinels are not much in vogue because in them there is no attractive colour like sapphire and amethyst. Meb Wilson's opinion is that the wine-coloured spinel is not so unattractive as to be undervalued. Contrary to belief it is an extremely attractive gem, in which two glows appear like alexandrite: sometimes violet and sometimes deep orange.

**[p. 121]**

There is also a spinel which appears blue in daylight and wine-coloured in electric light.

**Places of origin.** Spinel is found in Sri Lanka, Australia, Thailand and Burma. Besides, it is also found in Madagascar, India, Afghanistan and the United States of America. Balas ruby and the real red are found in the limestones of Burma and Thailand. It is a strange thing that in river pebbles spinel, despite its comparatively lower hardness, is found in the form of perfect crystals, while ruby is found in a worn state. Beautiful violet, green and blue spinels are found in Sri Lanka.

**Famous spinels.** Two large polished red spinels are kept in the British Museum. Near them a beautiful faceted blue spinel is also kept. A very large red spinel gem, which is oval, is set in the crown of England. Earlier it was taken to be a ruby. It is called the Black Prince's Ruby. There was a hole in the middle of this gem, but now it has been effaced by a small gem of that colour.

In the gems of the British royal family there is a famous spinel which is called Timur's Ruby or Khiraj-e-Alam. Its weight is a little more than 352 carats. It is the largest among the known spinel gems. It has not been cut, but has certainly been polished. In 1398 it was obtained as booty by Timur Lang in Delhi. With the decline of the Tatar state this gem became the property of the Shah of Iran. Later Abbas I presented it to the Mughal emperor Jahangir. It remained in Delhi till the invasion of Nadir Shah in 1739. At that time it was obtained by Nadir Shah in the loot along with the Koh-i-noor. Then it remained in the possession of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, and later came to the East India Company. The Company presented it to Queen Victoria.

**Artificial spinel.** Spinels are made artificially in fair quantity, colourless or in captivating colours. But it is easy to recognise the real and the fake spinel.

**[p. 122]**

Though artificial spinels are made in quite attractive colours, in them the colours of the real spinel cannot come.

### Garnet (Tamara)

Though soft, an extremely attractive gem: the four varieties of gem garnet: hessonite, almandine and uvarovite, pyrope: garnet, an extremely popular semi-precious gem: a 468-carat pyrope garnet: carbuncle (lohit pashan): almandine garnet: demantoid: garnet's attraction: on account of its glittering green colour the illusion of its being peridot (ghrit mani): the Ural emerald: soft, but with more optical dispersion than diamond.

Garnet is called tamara in our country. It is also called raktamani. The common belief is that garnet or tamara is a deep-red transparent gemstone, but the real fact is that garnet is the name of a group of minerals, under which many minerals come, and not all minerals are gemstones. In all of them there is the same chemical composition and crystalline property, but their physical properties are different.

All garnets are double silicates in which one metal can be calcium, iron, magnesium or manganese, and the second can be aluminium, iron or chromium. The crystals are found in the cubic system, and their form is round like a water-worn pebble.

Many varieties of garnet are not fit for ornaments, because they are lustreless and unattractive; such as (1) uvarovite, (2) spessartite and (3) rhodolite. The varieties fit for ornaments (of gem class) are (1) hessonite, (2) pyrope, (3) almandine and (4) demantoid. The gems of these varieties often appear alike, from which it becomes difficult to know the difference.

**[p. 123]**

**(1) Hessonite garnet.** Physical properties: hardness 7.5; specific gravity 3.61–3.66; refractive index 1.75. Speciality and colour: this gemstone ranges from golden yellow to cinnamon brown. In artificial light its colour looks more beautiful. The light-coloured stones are called cinnamon, and the dark-coloured ones hessonite. Sometimes these are also called essonite. Sometimes they are wrongly called jacinth (the real jacinth is zircon). These garnets are calcium-aluminium compounds, and by their distinct structure can easily be recognised. They appear made of granular dots, which can be seen without any microscope. Sometimes these red gemstones are taken for red zircon, but zircons are more glittering and in them there is double refraction.

Places of origin: most of the gemstones of this variety come from Sri Lanka, where they are also sold by calling them zircon or some other gem. Some gemstones, which are of light green colour full of black spots and look like jadeite, are found near Pretoria in South Africa. They are sold by the name "the jade of South Africa" (harit mani) or Transvaal jade, though there is no connection between jade and hessonite. In Brazil, besides hessonite, all kinds of garnets are found.

**(2) Pyrope garnet.** Physical properties: hardness 7; specific gravity 3.7–3.8; refractive index. Places of origin and colour etc.: these garnets are the most popular. Chemically they are magnesium-aluminium garnets, and their colour is from deep red to ruby-like red.

**[p. 124]**

Sometimes in some gemstones a yellow tint spoils their red colour. Some names of the gemstones of this class are Cape ruby, Arizona ruby and Bohemian garnet. These garnets are mostly obtained from Bohemia. These garnets are usually small, but gems of large size have also been obtained. The Emperor of Saxony had one such garnet of 468 carats. In Bohemia these gems are found in pebbles together with serpentine (zahar mohra). These garnets are also found with diamond in Transvaal, South Africa. In Rhodesia and some places of the United States of America too garnets of this kind are found.

Rubies have flaws, but the garnets of this class have no flaws, and because of their attractive colour they have obtained popularity. Because it is found in abundance, it is counted among the semi-precious.

**(3) Almandine garnet.** Physical properties: hardness 7; specific gravity 3.9–4.2; refractive index. It is sometimes called Syrian garnet or Adelaide ruby, though it is neither ruby nor is it obtained in Syria. It is of deep red colour bearing a yellow sparkle. It is an iron-aluminium garnet. The deep-red gemstones to which a cabochon cut is given are called carbuncle. The jewellers of North India call this kind of gem lohit pashan. A specially delightful thing in this gem is that seen through a spectroscope (the instrument for seeing rays), a magical view of coloured rays appears in it. In some gems a four-rayed star appears on the top.

**[p. 125]**

Places of origin: the garnets of this class are mostly found in the layers of mica mines. The crystals are usually of large size, especially those that come from Brazil. There they are mostly found together with topaz. Its other places of origin are Sri Lanka, India, Australia, East Africa and the United States of America.

A semi-precious gem: it is a semi-precious gem, though on account of beauty it is much respected.

**(4) Demantoid garnet.** Physical properties: hardness 6; specific gravity 3.84; refractive index. The gems of this variety are wrongly called olivine, which is misleading. The real olivine is called peridot (zabarzad, ghrit mani). The chemical composition of these garnets is calcium-iron. They are of glittering colours. Some stones are also brown-yellow and green-brown. Their popularity has continued since 1880, when they were obtained in plenty in the Ural mountain region of Russia. Still they are called "Uralian Emerald". Their most praiseworthy and attractive property is the scattering or dispersion of light. It is said that this property of theirs is more than that of diamond. In artificial light this gem looks very beautiful, but being extremely soft, in it there is little durability.

Places of origin: the garnets of this variety are still found abundantly in the Ural mountain region, and are mostly obtained in the river beds together with pebbles.

Defects: one, these stones are soft; second, they are obtained in abundance. So they are considered quite semi-precious gems. Like other garnet gemstones these come under the cubic system. In them there is single refraction and no dichroism. Yes, in some gem double refraction is also seen. These gemstones are given the brilliant, mixed and cabochon cuts.

**[p. 126]**

According to one text — excellent tamara appears like black ruby and like spinel, but the body of tamara is hard. The body of spinel is soft, and that of ruby is hard. Their specific gravities and hardnesses too are different. Tamara is heavier than spinel.

**The tamara of India.** In India these gems are found in many places, which are given below:

Rajasthan — the tamara here is of the excellent class. It is obtained in fair quantity in the rock layers of the Aravalli mountain region and in the beds of the rivers issuing from that place. Ajmer, Kishangarh and Jaipur are places famous for tamara. In Jaipur there is a good industry of cutting tamara. The rough also comes from Jaipur to Delhi. There too the work of cutting it is done. From here the material is exported to London.

Andhra Pradesh — near Hyderabad at Warangal tamara is obtained. The material of this place goes to the market of Madras, where the work of cutting and polishing it is done. The material of this place is sent to Europe.

Tamil Nadu — in this region tamara is found at Visakhapatnam, Godavari, Trichy and Tinnevelly.

Bihar — near Hazaribagh. Odisha — near Cuttack. Madhya Pradesh — in the manganese mines, clear orange-coloured tamara is obtained.

### Tourmaline

Chemical composition: an abundance of various colours: three varieties: separate varieties according to colour: amusing names — the emerald of Brazil, the sapphire of Brazil: two and more colours in one single gem: places of origin.

**[p. 127]**

**Various names.** Tourmaline is called vaikranta in Sanskrit, and turmali or shobhamani in Hindustani.

**Physical properties.** Hardness 7½; specific gravity 3.0; refractive index. This gemstone is obtained in many colours. Though its hardness is not high, it is fit for use in ornaments. Its chemical composition is somewhat complex, and in the composition of different stones there is also some difference, on account of which several colours arise in it. Generally it is the silicate and borate of alumina, magnesia and iron, and its crystals come under the hexagonal system. The crystals usually have three or six sides of rough-rectangular shape, and distinct vertical striations appear on them.

The three varieties of tourmaline are: (1) Alkali tourmaline — in it there is sodium, lithium and potassium. It is usually colourless, red or green. (2) Iron tourmaline — it is mostly black. (3) Magnesium tourmaline — it is colourless, yellow-brown or brown-black. The different varieties get names according to colour. The red and pink gemstones are called rubellite. The black stones are called schorl. The green stones are called the emerald of Brazil. The blue tourmalines are called indicolite or the sapphire of Brazil. The violet-red one is called siberite. The green or green-yellow stone is known by the name of the ghrit mani of Sri Lanka or Ceylon chrysolite.

**Colour, lustre and special properties.** The colourless stones are called achroite. And still more colours are in these stones. In truth, in the gemstones of the tourmaline class a treasure of colours is stored. Some stones are two-coloured; some are such that from above they are green and from inside red; some from above red and from inside green. Some are such that on one side there is red colour and on the other black or green. These gemstones are transparent, semi-transparent or translucent. The stone of the schorl variety is quite opaque and does not come in the gem class.

**[p. 128]**

All the gemstones of the tourmaline class have a glassy lustre and are pyro-electric; that is, when they are rubbed or heated, electricity is produced in them and they attract pieces of paper. The greatest and special property in this gemstone is its dichroism. It is so distinct that while cutting, attention has to be paid to it. To these the brilliant, step or mixed cut is given. In the brilliant cut there is special attraction. Because of its various iridescent colours this gem becomes the ornament of ornaments. When set among other gems in an ornament, they double its beauty.

The most glittering stones are those in which there is not even a trace of iron, and the most sparkling are those which bear a blackish colour. In many stones their dichroism can be seen just so. Places of origin: tourmaline is found in granite rocks. Rubellite tourmaline is mostly obtained from California, and in Brazil and the Ural region red and green gemstones are found. Yellow and brown stones are in Sri Lanka, and deep brown ones in Saxony. From the place called Maine in the United States of America come red, green and blue tourmalines. In Elba and Madagascar too stones of these colours are found. The gemstones found in Burma near Mogok are red and black. Here they are mostly obtained in the river beds. From Burma most are sent to China, where after cutting they are used in the form of buttons. In South-West Africa too tourmalines are found, but the stones of this place are of deep blue-green colour.

In India too tourmalines are found. In Kashmir it has been obtained with sapphire since ancient times. In the mica mines of the Hazaribagh district of Bihar it is obtained with mica. The tourmaline of Bihar is green with a blue tint. In the bed of the Kaveri river sometimes excellent gems are found. In Nepal tourmalines with a red tint are obtained.

**[p. 130]**

According to Meb Wilson, tourmaline has this property that when it is heated, on one of its ends a positive and on the other end a negative electric charge is produced. When the stone becomes cool, there is an interchange in the electric charges of the two ends. Sometimes it also happens that a gem set in some ornament touches the body, and even from the warmth of the body an electric charge is produced in it, and small light objects begin to stick to it.

According to Meb Wilson, in tourmaline there is a wonderful display of colours. Usually on one end of the stone there is one colour and on the other end another. Some Brazilian tourmalines, which are called watermelon tourmaline, are red inside and green outside. In some stones of California there is green inside and red outside. Tourmaline's beauty comes out with the step cut. The table cut and the cabochon cut spoil the beauty of this gem. If a bright red tourmaline is set with diamonds, both it and the diamonds gain grace. The blue tourmaline of Africa is of a glittering peacock colour. If well cut and polished, it can rival any other beautiful gem. An amusing thing in the gems of this colour is that if heat is applied to it, its peacock colour is transformed into the green of emerald.

Often other coloured stones are taken for tourmaline, but by its low specific gravity and strong dichroism its identification is not difficult. When green tourmalines first reached the jewellers' markets of Europe in the seventeenth century, they were taken for emerald, though their colour is different from the velvety green of emerald.

According to Kunz, at the place called Mesa Grande in California, where the tourmalines found are perfectly transparent, two large beautiful crystals of rubellite were obtained. Of these one was 45 millimetres long and 42 millimetres in diameter, and the second was 56 millimetres long and 24 millimetres in diameter. From Madagascar variegated tourmaline is obtained.

### Aquamarine (Vaeruj)

Aquamarine, a member of the emerald family: emerald, though flawed, is precious, but this, though flawless, is semi-precious: an eye-pleasing attractive colour: the pink variety: in artificial light the real colour remains: the largest aquamarine was of 43 pounds: the difference between blue topaz, blue zircon and aquamarine and their identification.

**Various names.** Its English name is aquamarine. In Hindustani it is called vaeruj or harit neelmani.

**Physical properties.** Hardness 8; specific gravity 2.70; refractive index. Aquamarine or harit neelmani is a gem of the beryl (vaeruj) class, like emerald. Its beauty is incomparable. It captivates people against their will. Concerning it Pliny's statement is that there is no other colour better pleasing to the eyes than this. That statement belongs to the time when he had seen only the low-grade green neelmanis of Egypt and the Ural.

Because aquamarine belongs to the beryl family, its physical properties are almost like those of emerald. Emerald is precious — flawed as it is, it comes in the class of precious gems; but aquamarine, though it is mostly flawless and though it may be called a gem of somewhat higher price, is still called only a semi-precious gem. Its chemical composition is the silicate of aluminium and beryllium. It is the same as that of emerald.

Aquamarine is a transparent gem. Its colour is blue with a yellow tint and sea-like green. By heat treatment its colour can be made deeper. The gems with a yellow tint are very cheap, but the clear blue or sea-blue gems are somewhat valuable. The excellent-class ones come from Madagascar, and in them there is complete dichroism. The yellow gems are called golden beryl (sunehra vaeruj), and the pink gemstones are called morganite.

**[p. 131]**

The stones that are opaque do not come in the gem class.

To these gemstones the step, brilliant or mixed cut is given, and they are mostly seen in large ornaments. In artificial light the colour of aquamarine does not change.

The origin of aquamarine is from igneous rocks. Its small crystals are also found in granite rocks. Its places of origin are Siberia, the Ural mountain region (Russia), Brazil and Madagascar. The crystals are sometimes so large in size that at one time people used to make the hilts of swords from them. Old carved aquamarines have been found, from which it appears that because of its soft colour, carving on it can easily be done. In Colorado and California (U.S.A.) aquamarines are found. Among them there is an abundance of blue, white, pink and yellow stones. In Madagascar, in the bed of the Sahatony river, the most beautiful morganites (pink stones) have been found. These are the largest among the pink transparent stones. Stones of such colour are also found in Sri Lanka and the United States of America. An amusing thing here is that on the effect of X-rays, morganite appears of the colour of a ripe cherry, and its lustre appears changed.

**Famous gems.** The largest aquamarine was found at the place called Minas Gerais in Brazil, at a depth of only fifteen feet, in 1910. Its measurement was 18 inches by 16 inches, and in weight it was 43 pounds. It was so transparent that everything could clearly be seen right through it. This gem was sold for one thousand pounds. An extremely beautiful specimen of aquamarine is kept in the British Museum. Its weight is 8.15 carats.

Often blue zircon or blue topaz is mistaken for aquamarine, but it should be remembered that topaz is harder and its blue colour bears greyness. Blue zircon is less hard and its polish cannot match the polish of aquamarine. There is also a fair difference in the specific gravities and refractive indices of the three stones, and if they are examined scientifically there is no room left for illusion.

In aquamarine gems, most value is of that gem which is not green but blue-green like the water of the sea. If such a gem is beautifully cut, it seems as if the waves of the sea are rising in it. An excellent aquamarine, if watery and glittering and set with diamonds, doubles the beauty of the ornament. The form of aquamarine does not blossom in the cabochon cut. For displaying its form fully, the emerald cut and the square cut are most suitable, and when these small gems are set between glittering diamonds in an ornament like a necklace or bracelet, the ornament sparkles.

### Peridot (Zabarzad)

Less durable, soft but extremely attractive in colour: for this reason its sway over ornaments: various cuts: places of origin.

**Various names.** Jewellers mostly address it by the name zabarzad. In English it is also called olivine. Its other names are ghrit mani, haritopal mani, chrysolite or the evening gem.

**Physical properties.** Hardness 6½; specific gravity 3.40; refractive index. Olivine is an important rock-forming mineral found in many places. It is the chief constituent of the peridotite rock. Peridot is a stone of several mineral groups, in which all are not of the gem class. Chemically these minerals are the silicate of magnesium and iron, and they are in the form of orthorhombic crystals and come under the orthorhombic system. The crystals are mostly not of good shape or form. The gemstones used in ornaments are green, and because of their charming colour they are used in fair quantity in ornaments.

**[p. 133]**

The deep-green gemstones are called olivine, and those that are green or light green bearing a yellow tint are called chrysolite. Sometimes the green demantoid garnet is by mistake taken for olivine. In the same way green corundum and tourmaline are called chrysolite. By the difference that is in their specific gravities, they can easily be distinguished.

In reality peridot is a soft mineral, and because of its low hardness it is not durable and is not suitable for setting in a ring; but because of its attractive colour it is set in rings and other ornaments. In these gemstones there is an oily form, but the lustre is glassy. The optical dispersion is also little in these gemstones. There is also a difference in the specific gravities of the different gemstones. In those in which there is more iron, there is more sparkle and also a deeper colour. It is a transparent or translucent gem.

In these gemstones the refractive index is high, but dichroism is distinct only in the deep-coloured gemstones. To these gemstones the brilliant, step or mixed cut is given. For peridot the step cut is more suitable.

**Places of origin.** These stones are spread in various kinds of rocks, but the crystals are so small that they cannot be cut into the form of a gem. St. John's Island, on the western coast of the Red Sea, is the only region where crystals of such large size are obtained that gems can be made from them. Here they are found as perfect crystals in the igneous rocks called dunite, but crystals of large size are seldom found. In olden days, because of the production of peridot, a strict guard was kept on the island of St. John's, because this island was the personal property of the Caliph of Egypt. Its other places of origin are Burma, Sri Lanka, Brazil, Queensland and the states of Arizona or New Mexico of the U.S.A. In these places it is found in sand gravel. It is believed that nowadays many gems of this class have been made by re-cutting old jewellery.

**[p. 134]**

The olivine gems of St. John's Island are quite splendid and their colour is beautiful green. Their weight, after being cut, is 20–30 carats and sometimes up to 80 carats. The weight of a rough gem has been up to 190 carats. The gems of Queensland are of light colour. The olivine gems of North America are of deep colour bearing a somewhat less attractive yellowness. The gems of Burma are just like those.

### Chrysoberyl (Krisoberil)

Among semi-precious gems one of the most durable: chrysoberyl a variety of beryl: alexandrite an extremely attractive gem: no bad effect of heat or acid.

**Various names.** For the keeping of the name, it has been given the name hem vaidurya, but it is mostly known by the name chrysoberyl.

**Physical properties.** Hardness 8; specific gravity 3.75; refractive index. Among semi-precious gems, chrysoberyl is a gemstone of fair value. Because its rough is obtained with difficulty, it is seldom seen in ornaments. Its one variety, alexandrite, is also found, but because its beautiful gemstones are rare, they sell at good prices. A gem of more than five carats is perhaps hardly found anywhere.

Chemically chrysoberyl is the aluminate of beryllium, and comes under the orthorhombic system. Its crystals are flat, and sometimes bear twinning. The deep-green gemstones are called alexandrite. The yellow and yellow-green ones are called chrysolite. In reality chrysolite is the name for peridot only, but the name chrysolite for the above gems of chrysoberyl has become current, which is misleading. Among chrysoberyl gemstones there are also some smoky yellow or brown-green stones in which, on turning and moving, the colour appears to change or move about. Such a gemstone is called cymophane or oriental lahsuniya. We have given its description in a separate chapter.

**[p. 135]**

**Durability.** Chrysoberyl is a fairly hard and durable stone. So it is fit for setting in all ornaments. In it cleavage is distinct, but except in alexandrite, in the other gems the dichroism is extremely faint. In alexandrite the dichroism is very strong.

**Special property.** In these the yellow-green tint is perhaps due to ferrous oxide, but its special property is that on the colour of these gemstones there is no effect of heat or acids.

In some Western countries the yellow gems of chrysoberyl are set in rings together with diamond, because it has a glassy lustre. These gemstones are mostly cut in the brilliant cut.

**The marvel of colour.** The colour marvel of alexandrite is worth seeing. The leaf-green gem appears red like a raspberry in artificial light.

**Places of origin.** The gemstones of the alexandrite variety are found with emeralds in the Ural mountain region of Russia. These are found abundantly in Sri Lanka. Earlier they were valued in Russia, because they were first obtained on the birthday of Tsar Alexander II, and after him their name was kept alexandrite. The second reason was that in them were the two colours of the then flag of Russia. Now this gemstone is not found abundantly in Russia, but whatever is found is of excellent class and beautiful colour. Because of the rarity of the Russian gems their value is greater than that of the gems of Sri Lanka. The chrysoberyl of the chrysolite variety is obtained from Brazil, Sri Lanka, Rhodesia and the United States of America. The most famous gem, which is complete in all qualities, is of 438 carats and is kept in the British Museum. Earlier it was in the Hope collection.

**Synthetic gems.** In reality synthetic chrysoberyls are not made. The synthetic alexandrites seen in the jewellers' market are either synthetic sapphire or synthetic spinel.

**[p. 136]**

These synthetic gems appear green-red in natural light, and in artificial light their colour appears yellow-red or wine-coloured. The specific gravity and refractive index of the synthetic gems called alexandrite are higher, but the specific gravity of synthetic spinel is lower than that of the real gem.

### The Gems of the Crystal Class (Sphatik)

In this class many durable gems of good colours: crystalline and cryptocrystalline crystal: transparent and curious-coloured gems: a king's favourite billaur which deceives as a diamond: smoky-golden like yellow topaz: the amethyst (kataila) of violet colour rivalling sapphire: the wonderful play of nature in lahsuniya and tiger's-eye hakeek: Lord Krishna, Mahavira and leaders.

**Physical properties.** Hardness 7; specific gravity 2.65–2.66; refractive index 1.54–1.55; double refraction 0.009; dispersion 0.013.

Under the crystal class come many semi-precious gems which have different names. Many are seen in well-known and cheap ornaments. The gems of this class, though cheap, are attractive and durable. In them there is the attraction of colours and of structure. Some of them, like hem sphatik (billaur) or colourless crystal stone and amethyst (neelam mani), have been in vogue since ancient times. Theophrastus wrote, "Amethyst was used in carving work, was transparent, and its colour was like deep red wine."

Though crystal is of many kinds and colours, its chemical composition is very simple. It is the oxide of silicon, that is, silica, and this same composition is found in all kinds of gemstones. Hardness, specific gravity and double refraction, though they bear some difference, such difference is also very little. The gemstones coming under the crystal class can be divided into two large classes:

**[p. 137]**

(1) The crystalline class — in this are kept the vitreous, crystalline and more or less transparent varieties of crystal. (2) The cryptocrystalline class — in this class the crystals are dense and homogeneous. They too have a crystalline form, but it can be seen with a microscope. This class is also called chalcedony.

(1) Crystalline crystal — these crystals form hexagonal prisms and hexagonal double pyramids. Sometimes they are bent on one side and sometimes on both. Very often twin crystals are also seen. These crystals come under the hexagonal system. The following gems are in this class: transparent gems — hem sphatik or billaur (rock crystal), smoky crystal, sunela (citrine) and kataila or amethyst. (b) The gems in which light appears to move, such as the lahsuniya of the crystal class, tiger's-eye. (c) The gems found in large sizes, such as rose crystal and aventurine.

(2) Chalcedony — in this class come the carnelian gem, chrysoprase, sard, jasper (ratuva), plasma, bloodstone (pitauniya) and agate (hakeek).

**Hem Sphatik or Billaur (Rock Crystal).**

This is a colourless semi-precious gemstone that is obtained in abundance. After cutting and polishing it appears a smooth, beautiful and extremely attractive gem, but in it there is a lack of brilliance. Even with the lack of brilliance, when its facets are made, in earrings for the ears or necklaces for the throat they look very attractive. Because billaur is obtained in abundance, only those gems are cut from it that are flawless. Besides ornaments, its use is in spectacle lenses and spectacle-related instruments. In the lenses of spectacles made of billaur the polish does not spoil for a long time, whereas in ordinary glass scratches appear in a short while.

**[p. 138]**

The prices of billaur keep varying more or less according to its demand, but it is a fairly cheap gemstone. The identification of glass and billaur is that billaur feels cold to the touch, is harder, and its edges (facets) are cut sharp and clean. It never appears completely empty inside as glass does.

Very beautiful ornaments, cups etc. of billaur have been seen in many museums. In ancient times it has been used in the work of making seals. Since the advent of glass, the carving work on billaur has become very little. At one time rich people used to drink water, sherbet or wine in glasses and cups of billaur. Spheres of billaur are even now made by cutting stones obtained from Brazil and Japan.

The most special property of billaur is the glittering of light in it. This is due to its structure. In spectacles billaur is certainly used, and because of its pyro-electricity, oscillating crystal plates are also made for radio and other electric-indicating instruments. Because of its transparency to ultraviolet light, its use is also made in photographic plates.

**Places of origin.** The rough of billaur is sometimes obtained in large sizes. Sometimes they are perfectly shaped. They are obtained mainly in the cracks of igneous rocks, limestone and granite rocks, in Brazil, Madagascar, Japan, the United States of America, the Alpine region of Switzerland, France and Hungary. From the Alpine region billaur has been obtained from time to time since the seventh century, and there such stones have come out whose length was up to 2 feet. The weight of some was from fifty to a hundred pounds. Some billaur has been obtained in England at Somerset, Cornwall, Wales and Butte. The billaur obtained there was given the names of diamonds. These names are Bristol diamond, Cornish diamond, Buxton diamond. Rock crystal (billaur) is also found in the northern regions of India — Kashmir, Kulu, Shimla, Spiti, and in the ranges of the Satpura and Vindhyachal of Madhya Pradesh.

**[p. 139]**

On account of the mixing in of some impurities, in some billaur stones a marvel of strange colours appears. In some, a rainbow appears on one side or the other. Such billaurs have been given the name of Irish quartz or rainbow quartz.

**Smoky quartz.** This is a smoky-brown billaur only, and the colours of billaur of this kind range from brown to black. The deep-coloured or black ones are called morion. Those that are brown-yellow and red-brown are called cairngorm or Scotch topaz. The stones of light blue colour are called quartz topaz, false topaz or Spanish topaz. In these there is no difference in properties among themselves. Most of the stones are deprived of beauty, but even then they are certainly seen in the jewellers' market. The colour in billaur of this kind is perhaps due to the sodium present in it. By heat the colour either becomes light or flies off entirely. Some stones become red or yellow-brown with light heat. This changed colour is permanent. In these stones there is dichroism, which can be specially seen in the deep-coloured stones.

Cairngorm is found in Scotland, and other kinds of smoky gemstones come from the place called Cordova in Spain. Some large gemstones were obtained in 1868 in Switzerland and are kept in the museum of Bern.

**Citrine (Sunela).** A peculiar internal structure — this is a yellow crystal. In it the yellow colour is perhaps due to the presence of some ferric oxide. Natural citrine gemstones are obtained in very little quantity, and the citrine seen in the market is mostly made by heating smoky quartz. Many people, seeing its colour, call it yellow topaz, but this is misleading. Citrine is a very ordinary semi-precious gemstone. Besides, it is a much softer stone than topaz. Its specific gravity is also very low. It is true that if citrine is cleanly cut, in appearance it does not look weaker than topaz or pukhraj, but keeping the two in front the difference clearly appears. Topaz is not as pure and flawless as citrine.

**[p. 140]**

One reason is that only those pieces of citrine get the form of a gem which are perfectly clear. These clear pieces, after being polished, appear flawless and perfectly transparent. Citrine is obtained from Brazil, Uruguay, the Ural mountain region, France and Madagascar.

**Amethyst (Kataila).** This is the most beautiful violet-coloured gemstone among the crystal gems, and perhaps also more valuable than the other crystal gems. Its colours range from deep red to wine-coloured, but mostly in these stones the colour is not uniform. That is, in one single stone at some places it is light and at some places deep. The stones are also flawed, or in them there are bands, and in strong sunlight their colour also fades. Some people think that in these stones there is artificial colouring. That is why their colour flies off. The real fact is that whether the colour is artificial or natural, in the strong light of the sun it certainly fades.

If these stones are given heat, they become colourless. Some become brown-yellow. Many citrines are heated amethysts, and in them there is no dichroism. In the natural citrine there is distinct dichroism.

Pale-coloured and flawed amethysts sell cheap. We have seen them selling at 2 to 3 rupees per carat in the jewellers' market of Delhi, but those that are deep-coloured and flawless get good prices. By "good" is meant from 5 to 10 rupees per carat. In the deep-coloured stones there is dichroism, and their deep colour is perhaps due to the manganese oxide in them. There is a great difference between this gemstone and the sapphire of the corundum class. The sapphire is far harder and more sparkling, and does not appear grey in artificial light.

**[p. 141]**

They are used in making inkpot-like objects. In olden days carving work was done on this gemstone, but now it is not in vogue. One reason for the low price of amethyst is also that in the last century it was obtained in abundance in South America. Before that amethyst used to be considered somewhat valuable. At the beginning of the nineteenth century the value of an amethyst necklace of Queen Charlotte of England was put at two thousand pounds. Today its price can be from 50 pounds to 100 pounds.

Its chief places of origin are Brazil, Uruguay, Siberia, India, Sri Lanka, Madagascar, Iran, Mexico, and in the United States of America the regions of Maine, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania, North Carolina and Lake Superior. The most beautiful amethysts are found in Brazil. Good gems are also found in the Ural mountains. The amethysts of India and Sri Lanka are also of excellent class. An extremely excellent amethyst of Brazil whose weight is 334 carats, and two Russian gems of weights 88 and 73 carats, are kept in the British Museum.

It is said that the largest crystal of amethyst was obtained in 1928 at Bahia. Its weight was 206 pounds and height 25 inches. By cutting it, many small gems were made.

**Lahsuniya of the crystal class.** This is a lahsuniya of the crystal class, which is softer and cheaper than the lahsuniya of the chrysoberyl class, but when to it the cabochon cut is given, in it there comes an effect like the real lahsuniya. In many of its stones artificial colouring is done, but these colours are not permanent. Its natural colour is greenish or brownish. Its places of origin are India, Sri Lanka and Bavaria. The gems obtained from Sri Lanka are well valued.

**Tiger's-eye.** This is a fibrous crystal whose colour ranges from golden yellow to blue. In it too, after good cutting and polishing, a light effect appears at night like the lahsuniya. It is a pseudomorph of crocidolite (blue asbestos). In reality it is formed from the decomposition of that mineral in which asbestos takes the place of crystal.

**[p. 142]**

In it the fibrous structure of its parent mineral remains. Tiger's-eye is found in South Africa, in the Asbestos mountains west of Griqualand. Its use is made in making umbrella handles, paper weights etc. One other companion crystal of it is of blue colour, which is called hawk's-eye. It is also found in the same place in South Africa.

**Rose crystal (rose quartz).** This is certainly massive and translucent to transparent. Its colour is light red like the colour of a rose. Sometimes it is also deep red. This colour is due to the manganese in it. In the transparent form it is seldom found. Its lustre is oily and it appears milky. In the deep-coloured crystal some dichroism is found. But its colour fades on giving light or heat. Rose crystal is cut in cabochon or in the form of unfaceted beads.

Large heavy masses of rose crystal (up to 8 feet across) have been found in the pegmatite rocks of Bavaria. Its other places of origin are Brazil, the United States of America, France, the Ural mountains, South-West America, Manitoba and Madagascar. In the Natural History Museum of Boston a very beautiful piece is kept, in which small and clean crystals of rose crystal are strung as beads on a necklace around a large smoky [crystal] column.

**Aventurine.** This gemstone is sometimes taken by people for jade, though its colour and chemical composition are different from jade. It is generally of deep green colour, and on account of this... [the reason] is these spots. In these spots there is a kind of sparkle which, seen through a microscope, appears metallic in lustre. Its colour is green-yellow and red-brown, but large pieces of the excellent class are not obtained. Its value is less than that of jade. Aventurine is also called Indian jade, because its cut stones are mostly in India. Some of its rough is sold to China, where it is much valued and there idols etc. are made from it.

**[p. 143]**

Aventurine is found in Russia, India and China. There is an imitation stone of aventurine which is called goldstone. In Hindustani it has been given the name of sang-e-sitara. This artificial stone is of red-brown colour, made from the mixture of molten glass and copper. In it points like stars sparkle, but it is so soft that a light filing wears it away or breaks it.

**The chalcedony group.** These are the crystal stones of the cryptocrystalline class, and are translucent or transparent. This substance is formed by the aggregation of small crystal grains, and its structure is fibrous. This can be seen with a microscope. In it there are different kinds of colours, and mostly they are pale. But because chalcedony is a porous stone, its artificial colouring can be done very easily. Its hardness is 7, and specific gravity is only from 2.62 to 2.64. A brief description of the chief gemstones of this group is given below:

**Carnelian (red ratuva).** Because of its pleasing red colour this gemstone is extremely popular. Milky white and light yellow stones are also found, but if they are not of excellent class they are unattractive. In the red stones light streaks or bands are seen. Some stones also have spots. Its rough mostly comes from Brazil and Uruguay. In India, Queensland and Japan, carnelian is found in fair quantity together with agate (hakeek). The cleaning, cutting, polishing etc. of the South American rough is mostly done in Germany. The colours of many stones are improved, because being porous, colours can easily be filled in or deepened by artificial methods. If carnelian gets too much heat, it becomes white ash. The colour change depends on the proportion of iron oxide present in each stone. Indian carnelians are mostly found in their natural colours. Their cutting and polishing also is done in India. A famous jeweller of Jaipur has a gem in which a pair of pigeons is made.

**[p. 144]**

We too once saw such a stone with a ring-seller on the road of Chandni Chowk of Delhi. Its owner was not ready to sell it.

**Chrysoprase.** Its Hindustani name is harit mani. Perhaps the reason for giving this name is that this gemstone is green like an apple. In it there are usually cracks and brown spots. The colour is also not deep green. It does not reach even the green of aventurine. Compared to jade (yashav sang-e-sam) there is much less shine in it. In it there is not even the lustre of amazonite. In it the green colour is due to the presence of some quantity of nickel oxide. Because good gemstones of this class are rare, in the crystal class it has become the most valuable. Because of some flaws in it, it is not very popular in the jewellers' market. In sunlight its colour becomes light, and by heat it is completely destroyed. Besides, these stones crack very quickly. Their places of origin are Silesia, the Ural mountain region, and in the United States of America, California, Oregon and Arizona.

**Sard.** In reality this is a deep-brown carnelian, and there is no difference between carnelian and it. Its colour sometimes takes on blackness, and sometimes spots appear inside the stone. Jasper is found in many places — North America, Germany, Sicily and Russia.

**Bloodstone (pitauniya).** This gemstone is not considered specially attractive. Carving is done on it. Mostly it is set in rings or its necklaces are made. It is an opaque stone whose colour is green, and on it there are red spots. From large pieces boxes or clock frames are made. Its use is mostly due to divine faith on it. It is considered the birthstone or gem of good fortune for people born in March. It is very cheap, but in fraud people charge its price.

**Plasma.** This too is a compact opaque green-coloured stone, on which there are long spots. Sometimes these spots are also yellow. It is found in East India and Germany.

**Agate (Hakeek).** This is the most important gemstone of the chalcedony group. In it there are mostly bands or streaks, which are sometimes indistinct. With a microscope they certainly become visible. Sir David Brewster made a microscopic examination of one stone, and in one inch he saw 1700 bands. In it milky, white, yellow, brown, red and many other colours are found. Blue and green colours are also there, but are seldom seen. The colour in these stones is not spread uniformly. In these stones the bands are straight, crooked and parallel, of both kinds. The agate stones in which there are alternating black and white bands are called onyx (sulemani). Those in which there are brown and white are called sardonyx. Those in which there are red and white bands are called red agate or carnelian agate. In this way, according to the colours of the bands, other names too have been given. Agate stones are mostly found in the cracks of the old lava (hardened ash) of volcanic mountains or the rocks made from it, because these are formed from the deposit of silica; in their layers sometimes very beautiful designs are formed. In the layers there is different porosity, so in artificially dyed stones several colours come. Deep green and blue colours are filled in suitable stones by chemicals. The colours are bright and mostly permanent.

**[p. 146]**

The dyeing of agate stones has been done in Germany for many years. This work is done at the place called Oberstein, which is the centre of the cutting and polishing of gemstones.

The chief places of origin of good agate gemstones are Brazil, Uruguay, India (South), the United States of America and Saxony.

When the natural forms of agate or hakeek are made, the signs of trees, birds etc. come in them on account of their shadow. In India this stone is found in the Tirni village of the Hoshangabad district, in the tributary rivers of the Narmada. In it stones up to 8 inches are found. It is said that in stones of this class, likenesses resembling Lord Sri Krishna, Lord Mahavira, Lokmanya Tilak and Dr. Rajendra Prasad etc. have also been obtained. And likenesses of several kinds of animals — such as birds, monkeys, snakes, pigs etc. — are also found in these stones. A wonderful play of nature is visible in these stones.

In India agate is also found in abundance in the following places: (1) In the beds of the rivers issuing from the mountain region of Rajmahal in South India, and in the rocks of the Krishna, Godavari and Bhama rivers. (2) In the regions flanking the place called Radok in Kashmir. Here hakeek is found with gomed and carnelian. (3) In the Santhal Parganas of Bihar. (4) In Tamil Nadu, near Rajamundry and in the Guntur district. (5) In Madhya Pradesh, at the place called Mogra Ghat on the bank of the Narmada, and near Jabalpur. (6) In some places of Gujarat too hakeek is found.

**Prase.** This is a translucent yellow-green crystal. In it there is an oily lustre. The blue crystal is seldom found. It is found in the Sikar district of Rajasthan. In the red crystal hematite is mixed. The imitation of the red crystal is made by dropping heated crystal into red ink. In ancient times this crystal was used in the work of engraving writing or figures. It is also found in Saxony.

**Heliotrope.** This is a deep-coloured crystal. In it there are scattered spots of jasper. Earlier people engraved religious texts on it, in which the red spots were taken for drops of blood. It is also called the stone of St. Stephen. It is also used in rings. It is found in India and Siberia.

**Flint.** In English it is called flint. It is translucent or opaque. Its colour is grey, smoky, brown or black. It is commonly found as nodules in limestone or chalk deposits, and mostly its outer surface is covered with a white coating. In it there is a conchoidal fracture. In ancient times this stone was used for producing fire. The tribals also made weapons from it. It is found in fair quantity in the chalk rocks on the coast of the English Channel.

**Basenite.** This is a variety of crystal black like velvet. It is also called kasauti (touchstone), which is used in the testing of precious metals.

### Turquoise (Firoza)

Semi-precious but sometimes also rare: in artificial light the colour of turquoise does not change: the delicate temperament of its colour: more in vogue in Muslim countries: the bestower of blessing: artificial turquoise: bone turquoise.

**Physical properties.** Hardness 6; specific gravity 2.60–2.82; refractive index 1.61–1.65.

Turquoise is called peroj and haritashma in Sanskrit, and turquoise in English.

**[p. 148]**

Turquoise is among the few opaque gemstones that are counted in the class of jewels, and its rare real sky-blue colour, which is seldom seen, makes this semi-precious gem valuable too. Its gemstones range from blue-green to apple-green. In artificial light its colour does not change. This is the biggest difference between artificial turquoise and real turquoise.

The chemical composition of turquoise is extremely complex. It is the hydrated phosphate of alumina, dyed with the admixture of copper. Iron also is usually mixed in it in small quantity, and because of some proportion of water inside it, extreme care is needed at the time of using and working on it. All gemstones lose their original colour after some time, and heat or warmth is extremely harmful for this gem. Many gemstones turn yellow as soon as they come out of the mine. Their turn to reach the market never comes. By temporarily burying in the soil the colour comes back again. To protect the colour of turquoise it should not be immersed in water or any liquid. Even sweat has an effect on the colour of turquoise, and blue gems become green. Its charming colour certainly attracts towards itself, but seeing the delicate temperament of its colour one has to say that this gem is not suitable for setting in ornaments. It is porous and also soft. A crystal stone can easily scratch turquoise.

Its natural structure is not crystalline. So it can be called non-crystalline or cryptocrystalline. Seen through a microscope, the fibres or grains of double refraction appear in it. In this property it resembles chalcedony. Its lustre is waxy, and this possibility arises.

The best blue gemstones come from Iran, where they are found in the cracks or cavities of igneous rocks. The Nishapur mines are the chief centre. These mines are very old. The mines of the Sinai peninsula have also been continuing for several centuries.

**[p. 149]**

It is believed that the Egyptians were acquainted with turquoise from 4000 years before Christ. In his exploration, a geologist, Major Macdonald, found some old mines, and from them he took away with him some specimens of the excellent class of turquoise, which were exhibited in 1857. The gemstones found in Egypt are mostly green and look like glass. The gemstones of Mexico are blue or green bearing a yellow tint. They are found in Sierra de Santa Rosa near Manzanilla. Turquoise is also found in New Mexico, California and some other states of the United States of America. Its other places of origin are Turkistan, Australia (New South Wales, Queensland, Victoria) and Afghanistan.

Turquoise gemstones are mostly given the cabochon cut. Turquoise has great vogue in Egypt, the Arab countries, Turkey and Iran. There it is regarded as the bestower of blessing. It is worn in rings. It is also set on the hilts of daggers or swords.

It is said that the best turquoise is in the possession of the Shah of Iran. It is flawless and about three and a half inches long.

**Artificial turquoise.** Artificial turquoises are also often seen. Turquoise is made from clay. Turquoises made of porcelain and glass are easily recognised. Bone turquoise, also called odontolite, is made from the bones or other teeth which are ivory-like in texture. These bones or teeth are dyed by natural means or by artificial process. The difference between bone turquoise and real turquoise is that bone turquoise has an organic structure, which when seen through a microscope is clearly discernible, but in real turquoise it is not so. Besides, in bone turquoise there is definitely calcium carbonate. Therefore on dropping a few drops of hydrochloric acid on it, bubbles begin to rise. Turquoise dissolves in hydrochloric acid, but in it bubbles do not rise. The specific gravity of bone turquoise is from 3.0 to 3.5. It is softer than turquoise. Its hardness is 5. Therefore in it there is a lack of durability.

**Variscite.** This is the hydrated phosphate of aluminium. It is also found among the green stones resembling turquoise. It is a very soft stone. Its hardness is 4 and specific gravity 2.51.

### The Gems of the Feldspar Class

The chief feldspar gems: chandrakanta (moonstone), suryakanta (sunstone), amazonite and labradorite: chandrakanta melting in the moonlight: the streak of light in it: the wonder of a curious play: amazonite, a green opaque but attractive gemstone.

**Physical properties.** Hardness 6; specific gravity 2.5; refractive index. Feldspar is an important group of minerals, of which only a few minerals come in the gem class. But they are not at all valuable. It is known by many names. Its chief names are moonstone (chandrakanta), sunstone (suryakanta), labradorite and amazonite. It is the silicate of aluminium and one other metal — potassium, sodium or calcium. The potassium feldspar is called orthoclase and the others plagioclase. The crystals of the first class come under the monoclinic system, and those of the second class under the triclinic system.

**Moonstone (Chandrakanta / Godanta).** This is a translucent, colourless gemstone of the orthoclase class, which is obtained as fine crystals of the monoclinic system. On these gemstones there is a milky lustre. When they are viewed from one face, a reflection of milky light bearing a blue tint appears on their surface. That is, a moon-like vision appears. The more blue tint there is in the gem, the more valuable it is. The stones bearing a yellow tint are very cheap, and those that are completely white are of no value. That is, they are considered useless. To these gemstones always the cabochon cut is given, and in it facets are never made.

**[p. 151]**

Their hardness is low and lustre is glassy and pearly. The cleavage is good, so there is no difficulty in cutting. Because of this moonlike vision, its comparison is made with the moonshine. There is some belief that the real chandrakanta melts from the light of the moon. We have not seen this in experience. It is also possible that the gem from which we made the test was not the real chandrakanta. The place of origin of chandrakanta (also called chandra kanta mani) is Sri Lanka. Chandrakanta is also found at the place called St. Gotthard in Switzerland, in Elba and in Burma. It is also found in India. The godanta (chandrakanta) of Burma is the best. The material of Sri Lanka is inferior to that of Burma. The material of India is inferior to these two. In it there are many colours, but compared to Burma and Sri Lanka there is a lack of elasticity, smoothness and shine.

**Sunstone (Suryakanta or Suryakanta Mani).** This is also called aventurine feldspar. It is a red gem in which, because of the reflection of light from the crystals of iron oxide embedded in it, visions of bright yellow or red colour appear. Its crystals come under the triclinic system. It is a sodium-calcium plagioclase. Twin crystals are also often found. Its chief place of origin is Norway. This gem is also found in Bohemia and Finland.

**Labradorite.** This is also called labrador stone. It comes under the triclinic system and is a gem of the plagioclase class. It is found in the form of large masses. Its colour is grey-blue, and here and there in it bright spots appear. These bright spots are very curious, and on moving the stones, in one single stone sometimes green, sometimes red and sometimes yellow light sparkles. When this stone is cut and polished in the form of a flat surface, it looks very attractive. Some look like black opal. Unfortunately it is opaque, and generally the gemstones of this class do not look pleasing. First this stone was found in large pieces or in the form of rocks on the seashore of Labrador.

**[p. 152]**

Later it began to be found in abundance in North Russia too.

**Amazonite or amazon stone.** This is potassium feldspar, and its crystals come under the triclinic system. After cutting and polishing, these green semi-precious and opaque stones look quite attractive, but in many good stones, because of white spots and cracks, their beauty is spoiled. In these stones too there is a moving streak of light. To these stones usually the cabochon cut is given, and in it facets are not made. From large pieces decorative vessels, flower-pots etc. are made. Carving is also done on these stones. These are found in the Ural mountain region of Russia, and in the United States of America on Pikes Peak of Colorado state and in the states of Pennsylvania and Virginia.

### Jade (Yashav)

An ancient gem: two varieties: jadeite and nephrite: colours as beautiful as emerald and peacock feather: China's precious gem: China's ancient beliefs: the body of the one who eats its powder does not rot: the panacea for all diseases.

**Various names.** In Sanskrit it is also called bhishma pashan. In Hindustani or Urdu it is called yashav or sang-e-sam. Some writers have kept its name as harit mani too. In English it is called jade.

**The varieties of jade.** Jade has two varieties: (1) Jadeite — this comes under the pyroxene class. (2) Nephrite — this comes under the amphibole class. Both come under the monoclinic system. These stones are found in huge masses. What is commonly called Chinese jade is in reality jadeite. What is known by the name of New Zealand jade or green stone is nephrite.

**[p. 153]**

(1) **Jadeite.** Jadeite has a fibrous crystalline structure, and chemically it is the silicate of sodium and aluminium, on account of which it becomes quite strong, though its hardness is only 6½ to 7. Its colour ranges from the colour of a green apple to that of emerald, and from green-white to white. Bright pink, violet and yellow-blue jadeite is also found. There are cheap kinds of orange, red and yellow jadeite. Usually this stone is opaque. Its specific gravity is 3.44, and in it there is double refraction which is up to 1.66–1.68. The most valuable gem-class jadeite is translucent and flawless. Its colour is green like emerald. The Chinese compare its colour to the green of a peacock's feather.

Many jadeite stones of the excellent class fall from their high position because of white spots or the colour not being uniform. In jadeite all kinds of gemstones, good and bad, are found. Some are of light green colour on which there are deep-green spots. Some are green like the leaf of a pea, and some are translucent green. Their value is ordinary. The white stones are of quite cheap price. But necklaces of gems of uniform colour have been sold up to three thousand pounds. The value of pink and violet jadeite is somewhat more, but not much, because people do not consider stones of that colour as jade.

The natural green colour that is in jadeite is perhaps due to the chromium in it. In some iron and manganese are mixed. Their lustre is oily, but the polish can be very good.

Jadeite gemstones of the excellent class are rare. In reality all the good material goes to China, where it is considered a valuable gem. There many ancient beliefs and faiths are connected with this gemstone. There jades of different colours have different names and values.

**[p. 154]**

Because of their peculiar fibrous structure, making some article on jadeite is a little difficult, but the artisans of China have been doing beautiful carving work on them since ancient times. Such carved ornaments sell at very good prices in foreign countries.

**China's beliefs about jade.** In China and Japan there is great respect for jade (jadeite). The Japanese word giyuk or tama and the Chinese word yu are used for jade and all valuable gems. According to the Chinese, jade is the root form of all gems and in it the inclusion of five chief qualities takes place; such as jin — charity, humility; yu — courage; ketsu — justice; and chi — wisdom. Their belief is that mixing it as powder in water, it works as a panacea for all internal diseases, and strengthens the two humours of the body, removes fatigue, and increases age. If it is taken in fair quantity before death, then afterwards the body does not decay.

The places of origin of jadeite are Burma, the Yunnan region of South China, Tibet, Mexico and South America.

**Nephrite.** Chemically this is a variety of actinolite, and is the silicate of magnesium, calcium and iron. Because of iron, in it there is a deep colour. The colour of nephrite ranges from deep green to white. Like jadeite, it is also obtained in fibrous masses. Its lustre is bright, and its hardness is less than that of jadeite. (Hardness 6; specific gravity 3; and its double refraction, refractive index 1.60–1.63.) By its physical properties it can be separated from jadeite. Nephrite is obtained in greater quantity than jadeite. In New Zealand, where it is called maori stone, it is found in the rivers like pebbles and in rocks. In olden times the Maori people of New Zealand sharpened their weapons from nephrite. Other places of origin are East Turkestan, Siberia, Mexico, Costa Rica, Venezuela and New Guinea. A large mass of nephrite was in the jade collection of Mr. H. R. Bishop, which was given as a gift to the museum of New York. In the Liton region of British Columbia and in the Silesia region of Germany too nephrite has been obtained.

**[p. 155]**

**Other minerals resembling jade.** There are some minerals also, certain coloured varieties of which resemble jade. The chief among them are the following: (1) Green grossularite — this is found in Buffelsfontein, Transvaal. It is called "the jade of South Africa" or Transvaal jade. (2) Xanthophyllite — this is a green variety of vesuvianite. (3) Bowenite — this is called serpentine jade. It is a green serpentine, and is found in New Zealand, Afghanistan and in Rhode Island of America. (4) Mexican jade — this is made by dyeing the calcite onyx mineral. (5) Green aventurine — this is a green aventurine mineral. (6) Saussurite — this gem is a variety of zoisite. Its colour is green-greyish. It is also white. It is quite a smooth stone. Its specific gravity is 3.2 and hardness from 6 to 7. It is found near the Geneva Deal. (7) Verdite — this is a beautiful green stone, which is found in the form of large lumps in the northern Cape river of South Africa. It is made up of green mica, called fuchsite, and some smooth clay-like substance. (8) Artificial jade — imitations of jade are also made from glass. These can easily be recognised.

### Lapis Lazuli (Lajwart)

A semi-precious gem of beautiful peacock-neck blue: the sapphire of ancient times: attractive spots of gold fly: a less durable but serviceable gem: places of origin.

**Various names.** In Sanskrit it is called rajavarta; in Hindustani lajwart and in English lapis lazuli. In the market it is sold by the name of lajwart or lapis.

**[p. 156]**

**Physical properties.** Hardness 5½; specific gravity 2.70–2.90; refractive index 1.50.

Lajwart or lapis is an opaque gemstone of beautiful deep blue colour like the neck of a peacock. Usually in it there are spots of yellow metallic colour like a gold fly. This stone is chiefly made of calcite, in which the colour is obtained from three blue minerals in different proportions. It is mostly obtained in the form of large masses. Sometimes it is obtained in the form of crystals. Such crystals are small and of cubic form. This gemstone comes under the cubic system. It is a cheap and attractive semi-precious gemstone.

The stones that are of the excellent class are of extremely deep blue colour, and on them there are no spots of any kind. Cheap stones are of light colour, and here and there have portions of white or grey colour. There is no sameness of colour. Large stones of good colour are obtained at the rate of two rupees per carat.

Because of its mixed composition and low hardness, working on lapis or making holes in it is difficult, and while doing so much care is needed. Its specific gravity is also not high. Besides, in it there is no cleavage. On the arrival of heat, many stones lose their colour, but on cooling regain their colour. Some gemstones become of faded colour by heat, which becomes permanent.

In ancient times decorative vessels, flower-pots, cups etc. were made from this gemstone. Their use was also in ornaments. Necklaces and the handles of knives were also made from it. In the churches of Leningrad the mosaic work of lapis is still seen. In ancient times lapis itself was regarded as the sapphire gem, and at that time it received great respect. Besides ornaments, painters ground it and made blue colour fit for their work from it.

The oldest mine of lapis is near Badakhshan in Afghanistan, where it is found embedded in the limestone in the rocks of a narrow valley.

**[p. 157]**

The mention of these mines was made by Marco Polo in 1271. Here even now the stone is extracted by the old methods. In winter days fire is lit near the rocks, and then water is poured on the hot stones. By this the stones become loose, and are easily broken off with blows. Most of its production is sent to India or Russia. Germany and China also take interest in buying this stone. Other places of origin are Siberia (near Lake Baikal) and the Andean mountain region of Chile.

### Amber (Trin Mani)

Not a mineral, a vegetable gem: the very first gem for the decoration of primitive races: the talismans of amber: the amusing history of its origin: semi-precious, soft and brittle, yet respected: varieties and places of origin: the largest amber: the wonder of nature in it: the portrayal of insects and leaves etc. in it: artificial amber.

**Various names.** Amber is called trin mani in Sanskrit, and amber in Hindustani, and also amber in English.

Amber is not a mineral substance, but there is a belief that the primitive races used amber for the decoration of their bodies first of all. In the Stone Age and after it, most amber was obtained on the coast of the Baltic sea, and it is said that from there it was brought to England by boats via the North Sea. It is said that amber was in vogue even 1200 years before Christ. At that time rich and poor all wore amber in the form of ornaments or talismans, because its colour was very attractive. From the many strings of amber beads found in old graves, it appears that the use of amber as a talisman was very much. From many ancient cemeteries of Europe, especially on the Asian seacoast, thousands of amber beads have been found. An extremely beautiful necklace of amber was found from a place called Wiltshire in England together with a skull. That necklace is kept in the British Museum.

**[p. 158]**

The knowledge of the attraction towards amber was obtained from Thales of Miletus 600 years before Christ. Plato, Aristotle and Theophrastus too were acquainted with amber. Isodorus, Siculus, Strabo and other ancient scholars mentioned amber in their writings. According to Pliny, "for objects of art the use of amber was made in an ordinary way." Homer mentioned amber in his famous work "Odyssey". In the time of Nero it held a respected position in Rome.

Not only in Western countries, but in Eastern countries too amber received recognition. The Turks, Arabs and Persians were among the connoisseurs of amber, and these people used the strings and necklaces of amber. In them the magnetic property in amber was a cause of great attraction. They regarded it as divine power.

There is the notion that the origin of amber took place in that age when the human race had not even been born. Countless years ago on the Baltic seacoast there was a region where there were such plants and trees as do not exist now. The climate there was warm, and in its forests tall trees grew. From these trees, because of excessive heat, a resinous substance came out. Later those trees fell from their trunks, broke into pieces, and sank into the bowels of the earth. When the era changed, the land subsided, and over the old forests, by the influence of the sea water, its form began to change; then a layer of blue mud settled on it, and amber is obtained in this blue mud. In the Ice Age other changes took place on the surface of the earth. On the seacoast of the Baltic sea, on the sandy layer beneath the sea, amber began to be obtained in this way.

According to this ancient history, amber is not a mineral, but of vegetable origin, produced from such trees as are no more. The colours of amber range from white to deep brown. Ambers of red, blue, brown and green tint are also there, but the commonly obtained colour is like the colour of honey, bearing a deep brownish tint. Amber is transparent, opaque or semi-transparent. Beautiful facets are made from the transparent class of gems. The chemical composition of all amber gems is not the same, but in most there is 78 percent carbon, 10.5 percent hydrogen and 10.5 percent oxygen. In it some quantity of sulphur is also seen,

**[p. 159]**

which in clear amber is from 3 to 4 percent, and in cloudy amber 4+ percent. The specific gravity of amber is 1.10. So it is somewhat heavier than water. Its hardness is only 2 and refractive index 1.54 (single refraction). From these physical properties it is clear that amber is neither a durable gem nor fit for setting in an ornament. It is brittle and breaks with a light blow. While working on it extreme care is needed.

Because amber is a poor conductor of heat, it feels warm to the touch, and it cannot be considered glass or a gemstone. Like interstitial substances, amber does not become sticky by rubbing. The electric charge that is produced in it by rubbing is negative, but on rubbing a magnetic power is produced from it, which draws light pieces of paper towards itself.

If amber is given excess heat, the oil of amber comes out. At a temperature of 100 centigrade it begins to soften, and a special kind of fragrance comes out of it. At a temperature of 350° to 375° it melts. On melting, smoke comes out of it and also a fragrance. Compared to other resin substances, amber melts at more heat, is harder, and dissolves with difficulty in ether or alcohol.

The amber of the first class is perfectly clear. In it there are no flaws, cracks or spots of any kind, and its colour is beautifully yellow like lemon or deep yellow. The demand is more for this colour, because large and flawless gems of this class are rare. They get good prices. Small pieces are very cheap, and the real fact is that nowadays amber is counted among the quite cheap gems. As amber grows older, its colour becomes deeper. Like old rice, old amber also has more value.

Its following varieties are there: (1) Clear — it is perfectly transparent. Its colour is white like water, or yellow, or brown-red. (2) Oily trin mani — the difference between clear trin mani and oily trin mani is that in the oily variety, because of the presence of bubbles of air, some cloudiness comes in it.

**[p. 160]**

In form and colour it resembles the fat of a goose or duck. The Germans call this variety by a name. (3) Variegated — in this variety of amber many small bubbles of air are found. It is cloudy. (4) Bony — the amber of this variety seems like a tooth or dry bone. In it there is little shine. In it there are air bubbles in fair number. (5) Chalky — this variety is white like chalk. No shine falls on it. It is opaque.

Some cloudy varieties of amber can be made clear by carefully heating their oil. In this process the oil permeates inside and also fills the empty air spaces.

**Chief places of origin.** Along the southern coast of the Baltic sea, in the northern and western divisions of the Samland of East Asia and of Königsberg. For centuries amber has been obtained from these regions. Here amber is found scattered in green glauconite sand. This sand is called blue clay. The centre of the mining of amber is Palmnicken. The centre of making various articles of amber is in Königsberg. In this region the amber-bearing stratum is found exposed on the Baltic coast. After a storm, the pieces of amber washed by the waves are called sea amber. In Palmnicken the mining of amber is done in an open-pit mine. First the upper material is removed. After this the amber-bearing clay is filled in carts and taken to the washing factory near the shore. Here the amber is separated from the blue clay. In this way 25–35 percent gem-class amber is obtained. The remaining amber is for separate industrial work. This gem-class substance is sent to Königsberg, and the rest is put in large retort vessels in the factory situated at Palmnicken and heated, and from it afterwards good kinds of varnish and lacquer are made. The amber reaching Königsberg is again selected, and from there it is sent to other places or various kinds of articles are made from it; such as the beads of necklaces, cigarette holders,

**[p. 161]**

gem caskets etc. Other such factories are in Danzig, Vienna and Berlin.

Amber is also found in Lithuania on the coast of the Baltic sea.

**The amber of Romania.** This is also called rumanite. It is usually of brown-yellow colour. Sometimes it is also obtained of yellow colour. It is transparent or translucent. Like real amber, it also becomes electrified on rubbing. Its chief place of origin is the fertile region of Romania.

**The amber of Sicily.** Amber of blood-yellow or light red to deep red colour is obtained here. Sometimes brown or yellow colours also appear. Its chief place of origin is the region near the mouth of the Simeto river.

**The amber of Burma.** The amber of this place is of one uniform colour. Amber of ordinary or yellow colour and of red or deep colour is obtained here. The light yellow amber is transparent. The amber of this place is called burmite. It is somewhat harder than the amber obtained from the Baltic sea. The places of origin are the upper tributaries of the Chindwin river, the Hukawng valley.

**The largest amber** was obtained in 1860 in East Asia (this region is now included in Germany). Its colour is very charming — weight 2½ pounds, and its value at that time was estimated at 1500 pounds. It is kept in the Museum of Natural History of Berlin.

**Artificial amber.** Many imitations of amber are made, from which it becomes difficult to recognise amber. These imitations are important, and it is quite a difficult task to recognise them from the real amber. The fake amber made of celluloid and glass is known to be fake by the mere sight of it.

**An amusing coincidence.** A peculiarity of the amber obtained from the Baltic sea is that in it sometimes pieces of insects and the shapes of leaves are formed. In clear amber this portrayal is clearly visible. Seeing this wonder of nature, an English poet wrote:

> "… a gnat, that in the amber bright, / Seems buried in the gems, and kept / For ages to the public view …"

Another English poem on this subject is:

> "… preserved in the amber stone, / The fly remains, and is not gone …"

### Other Miscellaneous Minor Gems

**(1) Malachite.** In Hindustani malachite is called dane firang. Its English name is also kidney stone. Physical properties: hardness 3½–4; specific gravity 3–4; double refraction, refractive index about 1.8. Colour: because of its charming green colour, malachite has been used for a long time in making ornaments and decorative objects. But it is a very soft stone and can easily be damaged. Still, being opaque, the polish on it is easy and good. Even in old worn stones, if needed, polish can be done.

**[p. 163]**

Chemically it is cupric carbonate, and its crystals come under the monoclinic group. In it there are several kinds of glittering green colour, and its crystals are of very beautiful form. In them the cleavage is complete. For this reason work can be done on it with a lathe, by which it gets a bright polish. In it there is about 8 percent water, which dries on giving heat, but by this process the colour becomes unattractive.

Malachite is found in many places, but is found more along with those minerals in which there is the mixture of copper. Its rough is found in Russia, Zambia, Chile and Australia. But the chief place of origin is the Ural mountain region of Russia. In Russia much craft work was done with this gemstone. Beautiful specimens of malachite can still be seen in the pillars of the churches of Leningrad.

Malachite or dane firang has a deep green colour bearing some turquoise blueness. In this stone there is a dark circle like a kidney. (The Western writers have not mentioned it.) The stone is smooth. Its test method is that applying lemon juice on a knife or iron leaf, this stone should be rubbed. On rubbing, if the stone becomes copper-coloured, it is of copper; if it becomes white, of silver; and if yellow, of gold. In this way, according to the test, three varieties of this stone can be made. Of these, the gold one is rare.

**(2) Spodumene.** Physical properties: hardness 6½–7; specific gravity 3.20; refractive index. A well-cut gemstone of such excellent class is considered rare. Some time ago this gem was unknown to people other than mineralogists. At the place called Pala in San Diego County of California, with the discovery of its charming blood-blue stones, it suddenly gained fame in 1903. Spodumene is usually a white, opaque and unattractive mineral, but its two varieties are counted in the precious gem class. The emerald-green variety is called hiddenite. This name was kept in honour of Mr. W. E. Hidden. He first discovered it in Alexander County of North Carolina. Its occurrence is limited, so it could not become popular. The other variety, hiddenite, is dichroic. So its colour changes according to the direction of observation. One of its varieties is called kunzite. This name was kept in honour of the gem-specialist Dr. G. F. Kunz. Hiddenite and kunzite are transparent, but their colour fades when placed in light. Kunzite is also found in Madagascar besides California. Blue and yellow crystals are found in Brazil. Two faceted gems, of which one is a beautiful kunzite and the other an attractive hiddenite, and whose weights are respectively 80 and 2.5 carats, are kept in the British Museum. Sometimes hiddenite is called "hiddenite" and kunzite by the name "kunzite".

**(3) Obsidian.** Physical properties: hardness 5; specific gravity 2.3–2.6; refractive index 1.5. Obsidian in reality is not a mineral but a rock. It is the lava of a volcano which cooled so quickly that its crystals could not form. So calling it the glass of a volcano would be more appropriate. Its chemical composition is complex, but in it mostly there are silica and alumina. In small quantity potash and iron oxide are included in it. In reality obsidian looks like made glass. It breaks easily like glass, and its lustre is also glassy. In it there is no crystalline structure. Its hardness and specific gravity are also low. Its colour is always deep — whether black-brown, grey or red. In some gems found in North America, streaks have been found. In some such substances were found from which inner shine appears.

**[p. 165]**

But gems of this kind are seen very rarely. According to gem science, obsidian has a spotted or streaked structure. In a microscope its minute crystals are seen, which are due to the stage of growth. From these, in the dark a play of colours is produced. Obsidian is transparent or translucent. In ancient times the people of the primitive races used it in ornaments and in making spears, arrows and other kinds of tools. Its places of origin are Mexico, Greece, Iceland, and the states of Wyoming, Oregon and California of the United States of America.

**(4) Sphene.** Physical properties: hardness 5½; specific gravity 3.4–3.6; refractive index 1.85–2.05. Chemically it is the silicate and titanate of calcium. For this reason its name is often called titanite. Its crystals come under the monoclinic group. It is transparent, and its form is like an envelope or leaf. Its crystals are mostly green. There are also crystals of yellow and brown colour, and after cutting they look extremely beautiful, because in it the optical dispersion is sufficient and its refractive index is also high, but unfortunately, because of the low hardness, that is, being quite soft, in them there is no durability. So this gem has remained unable to obtain a respected position in ornaments, though besides the other properties given above, in it there is dichroism and the cleavage is also good. Sphene is found in schist and limestones. Its places of origin are Switzerland, Madagascar, and the states of Maine, New York and Pennsylvania of the United States of America.

**(5) Soapstone.** This is also called steatite and talc. According to Ratna Prakash its Hindustani name is ghiya patthar or sang-e-jarahat. Physical properties: hardness 1–1.5; specific gravity 2.7–2.8. It is obtained in the form of crystals, and comes under the tetragonal or monoclinic group.

**[p. 166]**

Sometimes soapstone is obtained in the form of large masses. Chemically it is the silicate of magnesium. It is very soft, and feels extremely oily to the touch. The statues cut and made from it have been obtained from India and China. Its rough comes from the United States of America and Canada. A similar stone, which is also called agalmatolite in English, has been found in China. There its idols and ornaments are made.

**Serpentine.** Serpentine is called zahar mohra in Hindustani. Physical properties: hardness 2.5–4; specific gravity 2; refractive index about 1.57. This stone is really found in the form of a rock and large masses. Chemically it is the silicate of magnesium. Its colour is oily green. From it cameos, flower-pots, clock cases etc. are made. In Cornwall, where it is found, various kinds of ornaments are made. It is also found in Cyprus. Verdite, the stone from which cheap necklaces are made, comes out of the serpentine rock. Mostly stones of brown and yellow colour are obtained. Verdite is found in the Barberton district of Transvaal. Another variety of serpentine, which is found in India and Afghanistan, is called bowenite. In colour it ranges from apple-green to yellow-white, and looks like jadeite. In India ornaments, the handles of articles and other such objects are made from it. It is also found in the United States of America and New Zealand. Some varieties of serpentine are such that their use is in ornaments like gems. One variety is williamsite (green coloured), which is found in the United States of America; Connemara marble (found in green masses) and antigorite (deep coloured), which are found in Italy. Serpentine is very soft. Therefore it can easily be separated from jade — there can be no deception of its being the other.

Serpentine or zahar mohra is a compact, light, soft stone. The colour is of a greenish shade mixed with yellow. In Iran there is a place called Khatun, whose zahar mohra is considered excellent, so it is called zahar mohra khatayi. Its mortars, cups etc. are made. Its identification is this: if turmeric is ground in the mortar of this stone, it becomes red, and if a thread is passed through this stone and burnt, it does not burn.

**(7) Fluorspar.** Physical properties: hardness 4; specific gravity 3.18; refractive index 1.43. Chemically fluorspar is calcium fluoride. It is found in the form of crystals, and comes under the cubic system. Its crystals are large and of equal size. Some are 4–6 inches long. Fluorspars are found in almost all colours, but the colours are pale. Only one variety of it is fit for use in ornaments. It is called blue john. It is of clear blue colour. Earlier it was found in abundance in Derbyshire of England. Its flower-pots etc. are also made. Lenses are made from the colourless stones. The cleavage is complete, and the hardness is so low that work can easily be done on these stones. Though in them there is single refraction, in the green stones a blue tint often appears. Its places of origin are Saxony, Cornwall, Derbyshire and Cumberland in England. In Germany and Norway it is found in the lead mines. It is also found in the state of California of the United States of America.

**(8) Hematite.** Its Hindi name is also lauh pashan. Physical properties: hardness 5½–6; specific gravity 4.9–5.3; refractive index. Chemically it is the oxide of ferric iron. It is opaque, and in it there is a dull lustre with metallic reflection. It is an important ore of iron. Its dense variety is called kidney iron; by cutting, beads of this kind are made which look like black pearls. In olden times this black shining stone was used for ornaments. But because of lack of durability it has no respected position in the gem family. Its places of origin are the Alps mountains, Norway, Spain, England, France, the United States of America, India and Elba. Many carved stones have been obtained from the old tombs of Egypt.

**Meerschaum.** Physical properties: hardness 2–2.5; specific gravity 2. It is a soft stone, and carving can easily be done on it. It can also be polished. Its use was made in making cigarette or cigar holders and the bowls of chillums. Meerschaum is a hydrous silicate of magnesium. Its crystals come under the monoclinic group. It is found in the form of a dense nodular mass. Its places of origin are Asia Minor, Syra in Greece, Moravia, Morocco, and in the United States of America the states of California, South Carolina, Utah, New Mexico and Pennsylvania.

**(10) Marble.** Physical properties: specific gravity 3; refractive index 1.48–1.65. This is a metamorphosed form of limestone, and when it is pure, in it there are white streaks. Often because of impurities, streaks are in it. The onyx of Brazil, the onyx of Mexico or the onyx of California, all these are varieties of marble. In Argentina opaque green

and yellow stones, in which there are brown or red streaks, are obtained. In Algeria, Mexico, Chile and Egypt too stones of this kind are found. In India marble (sangmarmar) of many kinds is obtained in abundance.

**(11) Alabaster.** Physical properties: hardness 1.5–2; specific gravity 2.3. Alabaster is hydrous calcium sulphate, and resembles selkhadhi (gypsum). In structure it looks like distinct crystal. Mostly its colour is white, but sometimes there are red spots or specks. It is so soft that a scratch can be made in it with a nail. It also dissolves in water. Its places of origin are Switzerland and the United States of America. Its use in decoration is like marble. In Algeria too there are its old mines. This stone is also mentioned in the Bible, from which it appears that in ancient times it had fair value. Pflores is the trade region of this stone.

**(12) Gold fly (suvarnamakshika).** Physical properties: hardness 6; specific gravity about 5. This mineral is the disulphide of iron. Its use in ornaments is there, but not much. It is of yellow colour, and in it there is a permanent metallic shine which does not spoil for a long time. Its crystals come under the cubic system. This gemstone is used mostly in buckles and brooches etc. This stone, though quite hard, is brittle. It is obtained in almost all countries. It is said that in South America large gemstones of good polish were found in the form of mirrors in the old tombs.

**(13) Jet (sangmoosa).** Physical properties: hardness 4; specific gravity.

Jet or sangmoosa is petrified wood like coal. It is a deep, dense and black variety of lignite or brown coal. In it good shine comes, and its use is made in ornaments and in the form of beads as gems. Jet has a woody colour. It is tough, so it can be turned on a lathe. The use of jet in the form of a gem was done in the Western countries for the display of mourning, but now its popularity has declined. Jet is a poor conductor of heat. So it feels warm to the touch. Electricity is produced by rubbing it. On heating it burns, and a strong odour comes out of it. At some time its smoke was used medicinally. Its chief places of origin are the seacoast of Yorkshire in England and Spain. In France it is found in some quantity, and there it is customary to set it in ornaments. Though it is not a specially expensive stone, even then its artificial gems are also made. The artificial jet made of glass has a bright polish, but they feel cold to the touch.

**Sang-e-sitara.** A stone of brown colour like geruva. In it golden specks keep shining. It is extremely soft. Nowadays the real one is not found; only artificial material is found. In the real sang-e-sitara there is water and a green tint in minute form, which is not in the fake. Both the names "sang-e-sitara" and "auraz" are of sang-e-sitara itself. **Sang-e-simak** — this stone is red, and on it there are white specks. It is a very hard and compact stone. **(16) Prehnite.** Its discovery was made by a gentleman named Colonel Prehn. After his name only, its name became prehnite. It is obtained in lumps like curd. Its colour is yellow or oil-like green. Gems are made by cutting the green stones. It is a soft stone, and in it there is little durability. It is transparent or translucent. **(17) Rhodonite.** This is a pink-red mineral. In Russia it is used in ornaments. It is mostly given the cabochon cut. Its hardness is 5.5–6.5 and its specific gravity is 3.4–3.7. Its places of origin are the Ural mountain region of Russia, the state of New Jersey of the United States of America and Sweden.

Its places of origin are Norway, Sweden, the Ural mountain region, Switzerland, France and the United States of America. Rutile-bearing crystal is also obtained from Madagascar, Switzerland and Brazil.

**(19) Sodalite.** This is of deep blue colour. It is set in shining ornaments. Its hardness is 5.5–6, specific gravity 2.2–2.4 and refractive index 1.583. Places of origin are the Ural mountain region, Norway, Canada and the United States of America.

**(20) Sphalerite.** Its hardness is 3.5–4 and specific gravity 3.94. It is a soft, colourless or yellow, red, green and black stone. It is transparent or translucent. Its lustre is like turquoise or like diamond. Its dispersion is usually strong. Having so much dispersion, because of being soft, in it the durability is not much. It is a mineral that breaks easily. As a gemstone it is found in the Udaipur district of Rajasthan.

**(21) Staurolite.** This is also called brahmak prastar or the stone of the cross. These names were kept because of the twin crystals in the shape of a cross. Besides curiosity, there is no value of these stones. The pendants made from it are worn by clergymen. It is regarded as a divine gift. Its places of origin are Switzerland, Brazil and the United States of America. In India it is also found in the Dungarpur district of Rajasthan.

**(22) Willemite.** This is the silicate of zinc. Though as a zinc mineral it is found in several places, the light yellow gem-class crystals are obtained in the state of New Jersey of America. It is transparent or opaque.

**(23) Zoisite.** Its hardness is 6–7 and its refractive index 1.70 and specific gravity 3.12. In it there is strong dichroism. It is grey, green or red. Its pink variety is called thulite. Beautiful decorative objects are made from it. Giving it the cabochon cut, its use is also made in the form of a gem. Its places of origin are Norway and Italy.

**Scapolite.** Its hardness is 6.5, but because of its easy decomposition its hardness remains 5.5. Its specific gravity is 2.77 and refractive index 1.549–1.570. Its double refraction is 0.021.

**Western beliefs.** People are of the opinion that divine power is inherent in gems. Their use has been made not only in ornaments, but also in talismans. It is said that by wearing gems, freedom is also obtained from calamities and diseases.

The first gems of which man obtained knowledge were — amber, coral, pearl, ruby, crystal, onyx, amethyst and agate. At that time gems were also placed in the classes of male and female. There was also the belief that gems were dew-drops which, becoming hard through the influence of the sun, were transformed into the form of gem-stones. Crystal, people thought, was made from the freezing of water. Regarding the colour of gems there was this faith — that the various colours have their own separate effect; in the same way the effect of gem-stones of different colours was believed.

**[p. 180]**

Some people said that in the eastern countries, where the heat falls heavily, gem-stones are formed by receiving the warmth of the sun. It came to be a belief that however many beautiful gem-stones there were, they were all obtained in the oriental countries. This is the very reason that the gems of the best class came to be called "oriental," such as oriental lahsuniya, oriental topaz, oriental emerald, oriental amethyst, etc.

In ancient times there were also some such gem-stones in vogue which are now known by other names. For example, what was earlier considered sapphire is the lapis lazuli of today, and what was considered topaz or pukhraj is the peridot of today. For this very reason it is possible that the gems whose names are given in the Bible are not the same gem-stones which are current today under those names.

It is not proper to say that in the Western countries, people in ancient times were enamoured of the diamond, because their knowledge of it was obtained only when it reached the Western countries from India. In the ancient religious books of the Christians, the Book of Genesis and the Book of Revelation, there is a mention of many gem-stones. The first mention of onyx is in the Book of Genesis, but in the other religious books — the Book of Job, Ezekiel, Daniel and Jeremiah — the mentions of gems are so clear that at that time too sapphire, emerald, onyx, coral, pearl and other gems had considerable popularity and prestige. But as we have said above, the gem that was the ruby of that time is the carbuncle of today.

On the breastplate worn on the chest by the high priest of the Christians, twelve gem-stones were set 1400 years before Christ. According to the opinion of the famous gem-expert and author of London, Michael Weinstein, those twelve gems were:

1. Jasper — diamond (perhaps crystal)
2. Ruby
3. Amethyst
4. Topaz (pukhraj)
5. Onyx
6. Carbuncle
7. Zircon
8. Emerald
9. Sapphire (perhaps lapis lazuli)
10. and peridot

Another author, Mayb Wilson, has given a picture of this breastplate in "Gems," in which the gems are set in the following manner. The merchants of that time used to bring gem-stones from Syria. The robes of the emperors were full of shining gems, and the places of origin of these gem-stones were southern Arabia, India and Sri Lanka. Onyx, which is today considered an ordinary low-priced stone, was at that time counted among the valuable gems. According to the Bible, amber and crystal were very beautiful gems. In another ancient book a poet, confirming the firmness of his promise, says: "I will build a palace, whose foundation shall be of sapphire, whose windows shall be of chrysoprase, and whose four walls shall be made of beautiful gem-stones."

Amber was the first gem which the ancestors of man used in the form of ornaments. Many beliefs were attached to it. Regarding the origin of amber there were how many stories. According to the statement of Pliny, one belief was this — that when Phaethon, a Greek great man or deity, died by a stroke of lightning, his sisters were transformed into the form of trees which, on the bank of the river Eridanus, used to shed their tears every year. These very tears later became amber. Even today in the Western countries this belief prevails, that the necklace of amber beads is worn on children for this reason, that from it their teeth come out without giving pain. Among the Turkish people there is this belief, that by fixing it in the stem of the hookah, no disease is caused.

Among the Greek people there was this belief regarding amethyst, that by drinking wine in cups made of amethyst, intoxication does not rise. The Jewish people said that by wearing amethyst, bad or terrible dreams do not appear. The women of Rome wore this gem for this reason, that their husbands' love for them might remain permanent. In Germany also there is the belief that by wearing amethyst, there remains no fear of thieves.

Pliny says that the ancient residents of Rome used to hang beads of coral in the cradle of the child, so that there might be protection from all infantile calamities. There was also such a belief, that by wearing coral there was protection from the falling of lightning, from storm, fire and the sinking of the ship. According to the statement of Paracelsus, an ancient Greek author, the necklace of coral was worn around the neck for this reason, that there might be protection from all kinds of fits (seizures), from spells and poison. The Romans wore it in the manner of an amulet; they also used it in medicinal form. By reducing coral to powder and making it drunk with water, the diseases of the patients of the stomach and the bladder went away. Sir Hugh Platt has written that in coral there is such a quality, that when a disease is about to come, its colour becomes pale, and on the recovery of the disease it again obtains its real colour. For protecting from the evil eye, the amulet of coral was considered of great importance.

Pearl also had great prestige in the Western countries in ancient times. It is said that once when the Empress Elizabeth was the guest of Sir Thomas Gresham, wishing for her health, Sir Thomas presented pearls along with the wine.

Opal has been considered an unlucky gem since ancient times, as it proved extremely unlucky for the emperor of Spain, Alphonso, in 1474. It is heard that on his wedding day he presented his wife a ring of opal. Immediately after that the wife died. Then he gave that ring to the wife's sister; she also died after two or four days. Then he himself wore that ring; later he too died. If for the emperor of Spain the opal proved an unlucky gem, for the Queen of England, Victoria, the opal remained a fortunate gem. It is said that opal was her favourite gem and she had a fine collection of these gems. It was just as the proverb says — while one bathes, the son flourishes. Today, according to the Western belief, the opal is considered a fortunate gem for the people born in the month of October.

In the Western countries in ancient times, the ruby was considered a very useful gem. There was such a belief, that the ruby removes poison, protects from the disease of plague, grants freedom from sorrow, and prevents bad thoughts from coming in the mind. Even otherwise, for beauty it was worn with great affection. The residents of Burma say that the ruby ripens in the earth. In its childhood it is coloured, and as it obtains age it gains colour, and when it arrives at full youth it becomes of red colour. According to the statement of the famous traveller Marco Polo, the emperor of Sri Lanka of that time had the most beautiful ruby in the world. It was quite large and absolutely flawless. A certain emperor was ready to give the value of a city for it, but the emperor of Sri Lanka was not willing to sell it.

Regarding the ruby there is this belief, that when a calamity is about to come upon the wearer, its colour changes. It is said that when talk of divorcing the Empress Catherine arose, the ruby set in her ornaments changed its colour.

Regarding sapphire, the residents of Greece had such a belief, that by wearing it, the prophecy made about them came to be fulfilled auspiciously. It is also said that by wearing sapphire, immorality does not come in women and there remains no fear of ghosts and goblins. Pope Innocent XI had ordered that for strengthening their morality, all the priests should wear the sapphire in the ring. The statement of Saint Jerome, an extremely austere person, was that by wearing sapphire a soldier cannot be captured in war, and there becomes a possibility of peace in the war.

Regarding turquoise it is said that it used to give information of some coming calamity by making its colour yellow. It was also believed that the one who obtained turquoise in battle obtained happiness and good fortune. The statement of some people was also this, that if a man or woman makes a relation with another's wife or husband, then by changing colour it used to reveal that secret.

Regarding jade — not in the Western countries, but in China there are many beliefs. They consider it such a valuable gem-stone that if its powder, mixed in water, is drunk, it removes all the internal diseases of the body. It removes fatigue, increases age, and after death prevents the body from rotting quickly. For the diseases of the gums it is considered a sovereign remedy.

Regarding the diamond there is a belief, that because of hardness its breaking is difficult; but if it is dipped in hot goat's milk and broken, then it breaks easily. There is also a belief, that if it is kept dipped in the blood of a goat for some days, then in making its pieces there is no difficulty. By wearing the diamond there is protection in war. The emperor Nero used to watch the games of Rome wearing spectacles made of emerald. **Some prevalent beliefs.** Regarding the diamond, one belief has been this, that it is of three kinds — male, female and neuter. According to the qualities of rasa, virya and vipaka, the male diamond is excellent; the female diamond is middling, and the neuter diamond is of the inferior class. According to another view, the diamond is of four kinds — Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya and Shudra. That diamond which has eight corners or six corners, and just as the reflection of the rainbow falling in water shows the shadow-image of the seven colours, in the same way when the diamond is held by the fingers of the hand above clean water, in the reflection of the diamond within the water the seven colours appear and luminous rays shine forth; whose weight is light but which appears large; which is round, but whose facets appear separately and clearly; which is free from lines and points — that is called the male diamond. The diamond which is flat, round and somewhat long, which is endowed with lines and points, and in which along with these signs all the signs of the male diamond are found — that is the female diamond. The diamond which is of three corners, and whose corners are bent and round, which is large and not light in weight — that is called the neuter diamond.

**Brahmin diamond** — is completely white and free from any kind of tint. **Kshatriya diamond** — is white, but in it there is a slight tint of red colour. **Vaishya diamond** — is that in which there is a yellow tint. **Shudra diamond** — is that in which there is a black tint.

The deity of the six-cornered diamond is Indra. The deity of the diamond of white colour, like clean water, is Varuna. The deity of the diamond of serpentine form and of black-yellow and blue tint is Vishnu. The deity of the diamond of the tint like the kaner flower is Varuna. The deity of the diamond of the tint like the eyes of the lion and the tiger is Agni; but the chief deity of the diamond is Shukra.

Wearing the diamond of red and yellow tint is excellent for the kings, the political leaders and the high administrators. The diamond of white colour is excellent for the dutiful Brahmins. The diamonds of the tint like the shirish flower are the best for the Vaishyas and the merchants. Those of blue and black tint are the best for the Shudras and the employees of the inferior class.

For the women desiring a son, the white, pure, white-tinted diamond is auspicious. There is also one view, that the women desiring progeny should not wear the diamond.

The diamond is the remover of fear, the increaser of patience, and the giver of courtesy, inner vision and purity.

The Brahmin who wears the Brahmin diamond is born in the Brahmin caste for seven births. Being the knower of the Vedas and the Puranas, he obtains great prestige.

The Kshatriya who wears the Kshatriya-class diamond, being a brave warrior and a cunning statesman and a political leader, always obtains victory.

The Vaishya (who does business) who wears the Vaishya-class diamond, being endowed with wealth, people and the pleasures of women, obtains respect among the people.

The Shudra who wears the Shudra-class diamond (nowadays its meaning should be taken as the employees), being the companion of saints and ascetics, intelligent and a believer in philanthropy, increases his prestige being endowed with wealth and splendour.

The person who wears the diamond has no fear of ghosts, goblins, serpents etc. There is protection for him from fire, lightning, thieves, dacoits and the enemy. There also falls on him no bad effect of sorcery, spells and charms.

**Defective diamond.** The defective diamond causes the wearer many kinds of damage. If the diamond is endowed with white yava (barley-like mark), then from wearing it there is destruction of happiness and wealth. From the diamond with red yava there is destruction of the cattle-wealth, such as elephants, horses etc. From the diamond with yellow yava there is destruction of the lineage. From that with black yava there is destruction of wealth. From the diamond endowed with the thread-defect one has to suffer mental sorrow. From the defective diamond there is loss of physical strength. The rough defective diamond produces many calamities. The diamond in which pits have fallen produces diseases. The diamond is considered the gem of Venus.

**Ruby.** The ruby also has differences of colour. Brahmin class — is of the colour like the rose flower. Kshatriya class — is of blood colour, that is, like the colour of the red lotus. Vaishya class — is that ruby which bears redness, blueness and dinginess.

From wearing the smooth, clean, good-coloured, well-edged, watery and bright ruby, there is increase of lineage, attainment of happiness and wealth, and collection of grain, wealth and gems. It removes fear, disease and sorrow etc. From wearing it, the wearer becomes self-strong, fortunate, prestigious, patient, virile and fearless. In him comes brilliance like the Sun, and in his body there is increase of lustre.

From wearing a defective ruby one has to suffer many kinds of troubles. From wearing a "sunn" ruby there is sorrow to the brother. The "dudhak" gem destroys cattle. The two-coloured gem gives sorrow to the father and to oneself. The gem with red marks causes quarrels. From the ruby of smoke-colour there remains the fear of sudden electric heat. If the gem has four marks, then there is the fear of the attack of a weapon. If it is flat, it gives stomach-disorder and creates an obstruction in the birth of a son. If in the gem there are white, black or honey flecks, then disrepute is obtained and there is paucity in age, wealth and happiness. If the gem is full of pits, it weakens the body. In the Atharvaveda the word "mukta" is found. It has been counted among the maharatnas. The meaning of mukta is — the special object which gives freedom from the diseases of the body, that is, the special object which causes the attainment of moksha from the world. In our country there is the general notion, that by wearing the pearl in the form of ornaments, or for the removal of disease by taking its ash, there is the attainment of the three purusharthas — artha, karma and kama — and in the end moksha is obtained.

**Chrysolite (kataila).** If the wearer falls ill, its colour [changes]. On taking an object mixed with poison near it, its lustre becomes dim.

**Citrine (sunaila).** From wearing it there is no fear at night.

**Douposhata.** This makes the married life joyful. It protects from plague and from the bite of the poisonous insects. It decreases the pain of childbirth.

**Jaharmohra.** It is said that in its cup, poison loses its bad effect.

**Chrysoberyl (zabrazad).** From wearing it there is increase of wealth, and the happiness of progeny and married life is obtained. From making the necklace of the gem of the colour of the feather of Garuda and wearing it, epilepsy is cured. From wearing this gem, the breasts of the women remain firm, and the effect of the poisonous sting does not fall. If wine is drunk in its cup, then old age comes late.

**Jet (sangmoosa).** From wearing it, the terrible thoughts go out of the mind, and terrible dreams do not appear.

**Mouse stone (mous ratna).** From it there is attainment of contentment. There is no fear. Self-confidence becomes firm. The evil eye goes away.

**Jacinth.** From wearing it mental power increases. At night sleep comes well. There is cheerfulness.

**Tourmaline (turmalin).** From wearing it the intellect becomes sharp, and there is increase of knowledge.

**Red stone (lohit pashan).** If a patient of a contagious disease comes near the wearer of it, its lustre becomes dim. In every kind of journey it performs the work of a protective armour.

**Suvarnamani (gold stone).** In the old books it has been given the name of the golden stone. There is such a belief, that from wearing it there is no fear of ghosts and goblins at night. Terrible dreams do not appear. The gloom of the heart goes away. A wave of writing poetry or an article rises.

**Jaharmohra (serpentine).** It removes the venom of the poisonous insects and of the insects that crawl. They say that in its cup, poison loses its bad effect.

**Gold fly (sona makkhi).** From wearing it, a fly does not sit on the wound.

**Sifari.** This is a stone of the sky-colour bearing greenness. From wearing it there is attainment of self-peace.
